Market Update Los Angeles Transit Multifamily Office Capital Markets

D Line Opens to Wilshire/La Cienega, Jobs Cool to 115K, and LA Multifamily Occupancy Holds at 95.3%

Section 1 of LA Metro's D Line extension opened May 8, putting three new Wilshire Corridor stations within a 9-minute ride of Koreatown -- a structural lift for transit-adjacent values. The April jobs report came in soft at 115K, the 10-year Treasury climbed back to 4.38%, CBRE pegged LA multifamily occupancy at 95.3%, and Jamison filed plans to convert the DTLA World Trade Center into 512 affordable apartments.

Read Full Article →

D Line Section 1 Opens -- Wilshire Corridor Goes On the Map

After a decade of construction and multiple delays, Metro opened Section 1 of the D Line (Purple Line) subway extension on May 8, adding three new underground stations at Wilshire/La Brea, Wilshire/Fairfax, and Wilshire/La Cienega. (LA Metro) The opening cuts travel time between Koreatown and the new La Cienega terminus to roughly nine minutes and effectively places three of LA's densest commercial submarkets -- Miracle Mile, Mid-Wilshire, and the eastern edge of Beverly Hills -- inside a single transit envelope. Section 2 to Century City and Section 3 to Westwood/VA Hospital remain on track for 2027 per Metro's project page.

For CRE, the immediate read is on land basis and entitlement upside along Wilshire. Senate Bill 79's transit-oriented upzoning takes effect July 1, 2026 (LA City Planning), and the D Line stops now sit at the top of every transit-adjacent investor's map. Expect to see a wave of acquisitions and Builder's Remedy filings within a half-mile of La Brea, Fairfax, and La Cienega over the next 12 to 18 months.

Jobs Cool: 115K, Unemployment Steady at 4.3%

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on May 8 that the U.S. economy added 115,000 nonfarm payroll jobs in April, down from a revised 185,000 in March but ahead of the 55,000 Dow Jones consensus. (CNBC) The unemployment rate held at 4.3%, average hourly earnings rose 3.6% year-over-year, and federal government payrolls fell another 9,000 -- bringing federal job losses to 348,000 (-11.5%) since October 2024. (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics) The information sector lost another 13,000 jobs, continuing the AI-driven contraction that has erased 342,000 positions since November 2022.

Bond markets read the print as soft-but-not-recessionary. The 10-year Treasury, which had drifted to 4.27% post-FOMC, rebounded to 4.38% by Friday, May 8. (FRED / U.S. Treasury) Translation for LA owners: financing costs are stuck in a tight band, and the path to materially lower mortgage rates this year now depends on whether May and June payrolls confirm the slowdown.

LA Multifamily: 95.3% Occupancy, National Vacancy Tightens to 4.8%

CBRE's Q1 2026 LA Multifamily Figures, published May 5, recorded an LA multifamily occupancy rate of 95.3% -- up 10 basis points from Q4 2025. (CBRE) Nationally, the picture is even more constructive: net absorption totaled 78,100 units in Q1 (vs. negative 1,500 units in Q4), vacancy dropped 20 basis points to 4.8%, and asking rents rose 0.2% year-over-year to $2,217. (CBRE Press Release) New supply continues to moderate, with Q1 completions of 58,100 units down 30% year-over-year.

The supply story has a clear local angle. Marcus & Millichap projects only about 6,200 LA multifamily deliveries this year -- the lowest annual total since 2015 -- and CBRE's national renewal rate has climbed to 57% of all leasing activity. Operators are prioritizing occupancy over rent push, which favors well-located, well-managed Westside and South Bay product going into the summer leasing season.

Office Conversions Scale: WTC 350 S. Figueroa Becomes 512 Affordable Apartments

Jamison Services is converting the 400,000-square-foot World Trade Center at 350 S. Figueroa into 512 deed-restricted affordable apartments -- one of the largest office-to-residential conversions in DTLA history. (Urbanize LA) The project sits squarely inside the City's updated adaptive reuse ordinance, which Planning estimates could enable 43,000-plus new homes by converting vacant or underused commercial buildings at least 15 years old. Cushman & Wakefield's Q1 2026 U.S. Office report shows total office inventory now shrinking as conversions and demolitions outpace new construction, with national vacancy flattening at 20.2% and leasing activity at its highest post-pandemic level. (Cushman & Wakefield U.S. Office Q1 2026)

Closer to home, Cushman & Wakefield's LA office Marketbeat for Q1 2026 separately reported the largest year-over-year sublease space reduction in the U.S. -- a structural cleanup of post-pandemic shadow inventory that has been weighing on Westside and DTLA pricing. (Cushman & Wakefield LA Office Q1 2026) Sublease draining is the prerequisite to any sustained recovery in face rents.

Development Pulse and Regulatory Watch

Shopoff Realty Investments broke ground April 15 on Bolsa Pacific, an 83-acre redevelopment of the former Westminster Mall in Orange County set to deliver 2,250 housing units, 220,000 SF of retail, a 120-room hotel, and 15 acres of public open space. (Urbanize LA) Construction of the full project starts in late 2026 with completion targeted for 2030 -- a leading example of dead-mall-to-mixed-use repositioning that LA County is studying at multiple sites.

On the regulatory front, a small correction from last week: CalMatters' Digital Democracy tracker confirms that AB 1157, the proposed 2%-plus-CPI rent cap bill, was filed with the Chief Clerk on February 2, 2026 -- effectively dead for this session. (CalMatters Digital Democracy) The California Apartment Association had reported in January that Assembly Member Kalra would attempt to revive the bill, but it did not advance. (California Apartment Association) For now, AB 1482's existing 5%-plus-CPI cap remains in force through its 2030 sunset, and Costa-Hawkins exemptions for single-family homes and condos remain intact. The legislative pressure is not gone -- it will return in 2027 -- but underwriting can revert to the current statutory framework.

Three signals to act on this week. First, the D Line opening is a multi-year tailwind for Wilshire Corridor land basis -- the next 12 months are the window for off-market acquisitions before SB 79 entitlement uplift gets fully priced in. Second, LA multifamily fundamentals are holding (95.3% occupancy, sub-7,000 unit delivery pipeline), so existing well-located product is positioned for a stronger Q2-Q3 leasing season. Third, large office-to-residential conversions like Jamison's WTC project are quietly removing chronic vacancy from the DTLA pipeline -- a structural positive for any office owner staying in the asset class. Market Update Los Angeles Interest Rates Office Multifamily Regulatory

Fed Stays at 3.50-3.75%, Owner-Users Now Half of DTLA Office Deals, and Brentwood Multifamily Hits a Record $760K/Unit

The FOMC held rates again on April 29 with the highest level of dissent in three decades, Capital Group's $210M purchase of Bank of America Plaza headlines a wave of owner-user buying that now accounts for nearly half of DTLA office deals, a 61-unit Brentwood multifamily portfolio sets a West LA record at $760K/unit, and California's AB 1157 returns to push the rent-cap fight back into Sacramento.

Read Full Article →

Fed Holds Again -- With the Highest Dissent Since 1992

The Federal Open Market Committee voted on April 29 to leave the federal funds target range unchanged at 3.50%-3.75% for the third consecutive meeting. (Federal Reserve) The headline, however, was the split: an 8-4 vote with multiple dissents -- the most since 1992 -- as four officials pushed for a cut while the majority cited persistent inflation tied to tariffs and the Iran conflict. (CNBC) Fox Business reported the formal tally as 11-1 in favor of a hold, with Governor Stephen Miran dissenting in favor of a 25-basis-point cut. (Fox Business)

Markets are now pricing in just one 25 bp cut for the remainder of 2026, with a second penciled in for 2027 that would bring the funds rate to a neutral target near 3.1%. The 10-year Treasury yield slipped modestly post-meeting to roughly 4.27%, but commercial mortgage rates remain in the 5.08%-8.75% conventional range, with bridge debt still pricing 5.75%-12.75%. For LA owners weighing whether to refinance now or wait, the takeaway is clear: rate relief is coming in increments, not waves.

Owner-User Wave Reshapes Downtown LA Office

The most consequential structural story in LA office is the rise of the owner-user. Capital Group has agreed to pay roughly $210 million -- about $150 per square foot -- for the 1.4-million-square-foot Bank of America Plaza at 333 S. Hope Street, where it has been an anchor tenant. (Los Angeles Business Journal) The tower last appraised at $212.5 million in late 2024, down from $605 million a decade earlier; previous owner Brookfield handed it back after defaulting on a $400 million loan. (The Real Deal) Replacement cost on the same building today would run as much as $800 per SF, according to broker estimates cited by the LA Times. (Los Angeles Times)

The DTLA pipeline of owner-user deals is filling fast. The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power is in negotiations to buy the 35-story TCW Tower at 865 S. Figueroa from Manulife US REIT for $92.5 million, or about $129 per SF, with closing expected in Q2 pending council approval. (Bisnow) Riot Games bought its Sawtelle headquarters for $150 million last December, and LA County's 2024 acquisition of Gas Company Tower for $200 million (vs. its $632 million 2020 valuation) set the playbook. According to CoStar, owner-users now account for nearly half of all DTLA office deals, while institutional investors' share has fallen from 45% to 26%. The thesis is simple: when the spread between distressed pricing and replacement cost is this wide, large tenants with long-term occupancy needs convert that gap into permanent equity.

Brentwood Multifamily Sets a West LA Record

On the multifamily side, Marcus & Millichap closed the sale of two adjacent Brentwood apartment complexes -- 11911 Mayfield Avenue (31 units plus a nonconforming studio) and 11860 Kiowa Avenue (29 units) -- to a family office for a combined $46.35 million, or roughly $760,000 per unit. (Marcus & Millichap) The brokerage said the assets attracted more than 10 offers and closed in under 60 days, and that the per-unit pricing represents a record for West LA. (The Real Deal) The Mayfield property is currently asking $5,300 for a three-bedroom; Kiowa is at $5,100.

The Brentwood print sits against a constructive supply backdrop. Marcus & Millichap projects approximately 6,200 multifamily units will deliver across LA in 2026 -- the lowest annual total since 2015 -- which should cushion vacancy even as the broader rental pool digests slower wage growth. (Marcus & Millichap) For sellers of trophy or generational Westside assets, capital is clearly there. For buyers, the lower delivery pipeline is doing real work to support 2027 rent fundamentals.

LA Industrial: $1.6B in Q1 Sales, Negative Absorption

LA County industrial volume tells two stories at once. Total Q1 2026 sales hit $1.6 billion, up 19.7% year-over-year and 50.4% quarter-over-quarter -- a clear sign that buyers and sellers are finding price discovery again. (NAI Capital) But net absorption in LA County turned sharply negative at -2.8 million SF, reversing positive 1.3 million SF a year ago. The median sales price registered $297 per SF, down 3% YoY. The Inland Empire continued to track better operationally: net absorption was positive 2.8 million SF in Q1, completed construction was down 41.3% YoY, and asking rents settled at $0.95 per SF triple-net. (NAI Capital / Inland Empire) For investors, the takeaway is that LA County's product needs to compete with a much-cheaper IE basis -- particularly for big-box requirements -- but premium infill assets still pencil at premium pricing.

AB 1157 Returns; SB 79 Goes Live July 1

Sacramento is back at the rent-cap table. AB 1157, which stalled in 2025, has been reintroduced and would lower California's statewide cap on annual residential rent increases to 2% plus inflation, with a 5% ceiling -- down from the current 5%-plus-inflation, 10%-cap formula under AB 1482. (California Apartment Association) The bill would also extend rent caps and just-cause eviction protections to single-family homes, condos, ADUs and individually-owned townhomes currently exempt under Costa-Hawkins, and would eliminate AB 1482's 2030 sunset. Multifamily owners should track the bill closely; if it advances, it materially changes underwriting on Costa-Hawkins exempt product across LA.

On the supply side, Senate Bill 79 -- the transit-oriented upzoning law signed in October 2025 -- takes effect July 1, 2026, mandating multifamily-by-right around major rail and bus rapid transit stops. (LA City Planning) That timeline puts every transit-adjacent parcel in the city on a different long-run growth trajectory than its single-family neighbors.

What It Means for LA Investors

Three signals dominate this week: rates are sticky for now, distressed DTLA office is being absorbed by long-term occupiers at replacement-cost discounts, and Westside multifamily continues to clear at premium pricing on tight supply. For owner-occupiers with stable headcount and long horizons, the math on buying versus leasing prime CBD office has rarely been more favorable. For multifamily owners, the AB 1157 trajectory is a reason to revisit hold-versus-sell decisions on Costa-Hawkins exempt assets before legislative risk gets priced in. Market Update Office Industrial CMBS SoCal

Discount-to-2019 Office Sales Hit Pasadena as Industrial Rents Ease: Fed Cut Timing and CMBS Stress Frame LA CRE Pricing

A major Pasadena office trade at roughly half its 2019 price highlights how far LA office repricing has gone, while industrial fundamentals show early signs of stabilization but softer rents. Meanwhile, Wall Street continues to push the first Fed cut deeper into 2026 amid oil-driven inflation risk, and CMBS delinquencies remain elevated—keeping debt costs and refinancing outcomes highly property- and sponsor-specific.

Read Full Article →

The Week in One View (Key Takeaways)

Los Angeles-area pricing is increasingly set at the intersection of (1) real-time transaction comps that reset to today’s higher debt costs, (2) a rate path that remains “higher for longer,” and (3) credit markets that are still selective—especially where cash flows are uncertain.

This week’s clearest signals: a Pasadena office sale at a steep discount to its 2019 basis, industrial metrics that look more stable on absorption but softer on rent, and a CMBS delinquency backdrop that continues to pressure refinancing outcomes for weaker assets.

1) A Pasadena Office Sale Prices in the “New Math”

A 159,000-square-foot Class A, LEED Gold office building at 35 N. Lake Ave. in Pasadena sold for about $33 million to the California State Compensation Insurance Fund. (Bisnow)

The seller, Swift Real Estate Partners, reportedly bought the asset for roughly $58 million in 2019—implying an approximately $25 million value decline (about a 50% discount to the 2019 basis). (Bisnow)

Two takeaways matter for investors: First, owner-users (or long-duration holders) can be the marginal buyer in office because they can underwrite value beyond near-term leasing volatility. Second, the size of the discount illustrates how cap rates and underwriting assumptions have adjusted to higher financing costs and uncertain demand.

What it means for CRE:

  • Expect office price discovery to continue “comp-by-comp,” with the best clarity coming from motivated sellers and nontraditional buyers (owner-users, strategic capital).
  • Refinance risk remains the central variable: assets with near-term maturities will be forced to reconcile today’s debt constants with still-uncertain tenant demand.

2) LA Industrial: Stabilizing Absorption, Softer Rents

CBRE reports that Greater Los Angeles industrial net absorption turned positive at 934,025 square feet in Q1 2026—its first positive quarter since 2022—suggesting demand is stabilizing. (CBRE)

At the same time, vacancy increased to 5.4% and availability rose to 8.1%, while asking lease rates declined to $1.21 NNN per square foot per month. (CBRE)

This combination—improving absorption but more supply/space on the market—often shows up in a “late-cycle normalization” phase: occupiers still transact, but the balance of power is less one-sided than it was in the ultra-tight 2021–2022 period.

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwriting should assume more tenant leverage than the last cycle: longer downtime, higher concessions, and more selective rent growth.
  • Industrial remains one of LA’s most durable property types, but near-term NOI growth is likely to be more operational (asset management) than purely market-driven.

3) Fed Cut Timing: Still the Swing Factor for Cap Rates (But Not the Only One)

A Reuters factbox published by Investing.com shows several major brokerages still expect rate cuts in 2026, with Goldman Sachs pushing its first cut expectation to September (from June) amid higher oil prices and renewed inflation risks tied to conflict in the Middle East. (Investing.com / Reuters)

The same factbox summarizes a range of 2026 cut paths—e.g., Citi expects 75 bps of cuts (three moves) while multiple banks cluster around 50 bps total—highlighting that the market’s debate is increasingly about timing and persistence of inflation rather than a rapid easing cycle. (Investing.com / Reuters)

For CRE, the implication is straightforward: waiting for “the cut” is not a strategy. The more relevant question is whether your deal works at today’s debt costs—and how much optionality you have if the cut arrives later (or slower) than expected.

What it means for CRE:

  • Cap rate compression is unlikely to be broad-based until there is clearer evidence that policy rates can move lower without re-igniting inflation.
  • Deals that pencil today (with conservative exit assumptions) will tend to be the best risk-adjusted opportunities if/when the cycle turns.

4) CMBS Delinquencies: Elevated Stress Keeps Refinancing Outcomes Uneven

Trepp reports the overall CMBS delinquency rate increased 41 basis points to 7.55% in March 2026, reversing February’s decline. (Trepp)

By property type, Trepp notes delinquency rates rose across most major categories; office delinquencies increased (up 51 bps), and multifamily delinquencies also increased (up 30 bps). (Trepp)

The message for owners and buyers is not that “everything is distressed,” but that refinancing is bifurcated: strong sponsorship and durable cash flows can still find capital, while transitional or structurally challenged assets face more limited execution options.

What it means for CRE:

  • Expect continued extensions, modifications, and selective note sales—especially where leasing risk and maturity walls overlap.
  • Investors with patient capital and operating capability may find better basis via structured solutions (preferred equity, rescue capital, or buying loans) than through fully marketed fee-simple sales.

5) Construction Costs Keep a Floor Under Replacement Cost (Even as Demand Shifts)

Turner Construction’s Q1 2026 Turner Building Cost Index rose to 1530, up 1.32% quarter-over-quarter and 4.87% year-over-year. (Turner Construction)

Turner highlights continued demand from data centers, manufacturing, and semiconductor facilities as notable drivers—factors that can keep labor and certain materials tight even if other commercial segments cool. (Turner Construction)

For LA investors, the practical takeaway is that replacement cost remains high, which can support existing asset values over time—particularly for well-located industrial and infill multifamily—yet the feasibility bar for new projects stays elevated due to both financing costs and construction inputs.

What it means for CRE:

  • New supply risk may stay muted in many submarkets because fewer projects can clear today’s all-in cost of capital.
  • Value-add and repositioning strategies can look relatively more attractive when ground-up pencils are challenged.

What This Means for LA Investors (By Property Type)

  • Industrial: Expect steadier leasing volume, but underwrite more competition (higher vacancy/availability) and slower rent growth near term. Prioritize functional infill product, credit tenants, and rollover schedules that avoid “all-at-once” risk.
  • Multifamily: Elevated financing costs and any increase in delinquencies in securitized markets suggest underwriting discipline remains essential. Focus on submarkets with durable household formation, and structure reserves for insurance, repairs, and turnover.
  • Retail: Neighborhood retail tied to daily needs may continue to trade more rationally than discretionary concepts, but debt proceeds will still be driven by in-place cash flow. Favor centers with necessity tenancy, strong co-tenancy, and clear redevelopment optionality.
  • Office: The Pasadena comp underscores that office repricing is real and can be severe versus 2019—especially if leasing is uncertain or maturities are near. Look for deals where basis is reset, capital structure is conservative, and there is a clear demand story (medical/owner-user, boutique creative, transit-adjacent value).

Market Update Interest Rates Office Industrial CMBS

Fed Cuts Get Pushed Out as Energy-Driven Inflation Bites: What It Means for LA Office, Industrial, and CMBS Risk

A fresh Reuters poll suggests the Fed may stay on hold longer as energy prices lift inflation expectations. At the same time, CRE debt markets are sending mixed signals: permanent loan spreads have tightened even as CMBS delinquencies re-accelerate, and SoCal industrial shows stronger leasing activity alongside rising vacancy.

Read Full Article →

1) Rates: “Higher-for-Longer” Risk Returns via Energy-Driven Inflation

A new Reuters poll argues the next leg of rate relief may be slower than many borrowers penciled into 2026 underwriting, with economists now expecting the Fed to wait at least six months before cutting this year as energy costs rise amid renewed geopolitical conflict. (Reuters)

The same poll highlights how quickly expectations can move: a narrow majority of economists surveyed still saw the policy rate staying in roughly the current range by the end of September, and nearly a third anticipated no cuts this year. (Reuters) For real estate, this matters less as a headline than as a reminder that inflation regime risk has not disappeared.

What it means for CRE:

  • Refinance underwriting should assume “sticky” base rates in addition to a conservative exit cap rate.
  • Deals that only clear with a near-term rate-cut narrative remain the most fragile.

2) Debt Markets: Spreads Improve, Even If Base Rates Don’t

While the Fed may be slower to cut, private-market credit pricing has improved. CRED iQ data cited by Commercial Observer shows spreads over the 10-year Treasury for 60–65% LTV permanent CRE loans tightening across the major sectors by 12–18 bps over the past year. (Commercial Observer)

As of March 31, 2026, spreads were about 154 bps for multifamily, 162 bps for industrial, 176 bps for retail, and 220 bps for office. (Commercial Observer) With the 10-year Treasury around 4.25% in early April, that implied coupons around 5.8%–6.5% depending on property type—still not “cheap,” but meaningfully more executable than last year’s peak uncertainty. (Commercial Observer)

What it means for CRE:

  • The market’s message is not “rates are falling,” but “risk premiums are normalizing” for better assets.
  • Sponsors with 2026 maturities should treat today as a realistic window to refinance, not a reason to wait.

3) Distress Watch: CMBS Delinquencies Re-Accelerate (Lodging + Office + Multifamily)

Even with spread compression, securitized credit stress is still building in pockets. Trepp reports the CMBS delinquency rate rose 41 bps to 7.55% in March 2026, reversing February’s decline. (Trepp)

Lodging was the biggest mover (+137 bps to 7.31%), with office up 51 bps and multifamily up 30 bps (industrial dipped slightly). (Trepp) Notably, nearly $5.1B became newly delinquent in March, and Trepp notes that roughly 40% of the largest newly delinquent loans had been “performing matured balloons” the month prior—suggesting extensions are rolling into payment trouble. (Trepp)

What it means for CRE:

  • “Extend-and-pretend” is not a permanent strategy; more loans will tip into delinquency as cash flow, insurance, and capex collide with higher debt service.
  • Expect selective buying opportunities, but also more appraisal-driven equity checks at refinance.

4) LA Office: A Large DTLA Lease Is a Signal, Not a Turnaround

Los Angeles office continues to show bifurcation: transactional “green shoots” show up in leasing and select tenant-driven commitments, while valuations and defaults remain under pressure.

The Real Deal reports On Location LA signed roughly 108,000 square feet (six floors) at 445 S. Figueroa (Union Bank Plaza), describing it as Downtown LA’s largest new office transaction since late 2024 (per CBRE Research). (The Real Deal) The building’s 2024 sale price—$80 million, or roughly $100 per square foot—underscores how far price discovery has reset from replacement cost. (The Real Deal)

What it means for CRE:

  • A “headline lease” helps leasing comps and momentum, but it doesn’t fix the capital stack problems for challenged assets.
  • In office, the investable strategy remains: buy basis, fund targeted capex, and underwrite longer lease-up timelines.

5) SoCal Industrial: Vacancy Up, Leasing Strong—A Market in Rebalancing

Industrial remains the healthiest major property type, but the SoCal story is no longer one-directional. CBRE’s Inland Empire figures show IE Core vacancy rose 70 bps to 7.8% in Q1 2026, driven by significant move-outs in 500,000+ square foot buildings. (CBRE)

At the same time, leasing volume was strong: 13.6 million square feet of new deals were signed in Q1 (up 40.2% from Q4 2025), and asking and taking rates stayed firm around $1.09 and $1.08 NNN per square foot per month, respectively. (CBRE) New construction also restarted, with 854,000 square feet breaking ground in Q1. (CBRE)

What it means for CRE:

  • The sector looks less like a shortage market and more like a negotiated market—especially for big-box space.
  • Underwrite tenant improvement and downtime conservatively for older, functionally obsolete product.

6) Energy Shock: Gas Back Near $4 Adds Another Constraint on Rate Relief

Energy costs are becoming a macro variable again. Reuters reports U.S. gasoline prices around $4 per gallon—roughly $1 higher since the onset of the conflict—keeping inflation pressure visible to consumers and policymakers. (Reuters)

In practice, this increases “higher-for-longer” probability even if core inflation trends improve, because the Fed is watching inflation expectations as much as inflation prints. (Reuters)

What it means for CRE:

  • Refinance risk is not only a CRE variable; it’s tied to geopolitics, energy, and headline inflation.
  • Investors should stress test debt service at current rates and assume fewer near-term policy offsets.

What This Means for LA Investors (By Property Type)

  • Multifamily: Spreads suggest financing is more available for stabilized assets, but underwrite conservatively because CMBS multifamily delinquencies are rising and operating costs remain elevated. Favor submarkets with durable demand and limited near-term supply, and keep realistic assumptions on rent growth.
  • Industrial: Inland Empire vacancy is rising, but leasing volume and rents remain resilient—expect more negotiation leverage for tenants, especially in 500K+ SF blocks. Focus on modern clear heights, efficient truck courts, and freeway access; underwrite rollover/lease-up with more time.
  • Retail: Debt pricing has improved relative to last year, supporting acquisitions of well-located necessity and service-heavy centers. Underwrite tenant health carefully; prioritize daily-needs and infill trade areas.
  • Office: Large DTLA leases are encouraging signals, but the reset in price per square foot and ongoing distress argue for selective, basis-driven underwriting. Best risk-adjusted opportunities remain recapitalizations, note purchases, and core locations where leasing demand is most defensible.

Executive Summary

Downtown L.A. just posted Southern California’s largest office lease so far this year, with Olympics hospitality provider On Location taking 108,272 square feet at Union Bank Plaza. (Commercial Observer)

At the same time, the macro picture remains unsettled: Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a swift end to the war could keep rate cuts on the table later this year, but warned prolonged conflict and sustained energy price pressure could complicate inflation progress. (Reuters)

Credit is not getting easier yet. Trepp reported the CMBS delinquency rate rose 41 basis points to 7.55% in March, with office delinquencies rising again and multifamily also ticking higher. (Trepp)

Meanwhile, development feasibility continues to tighten: Cushman & Wakefield estimates tariffs as of April 7 are lifting materials costs about 6% versus a 2024 baseline, translating to roughly 3% higher total project costs. (Cushman & Wakefield)

1) Downtown L.A. Gets a Demand Signal: 108K SF at Union Bank Plaza

On Location, the official hospitality provider for the 2028 Summer Olympics, signed a 108,272-square-foot lease at Union Bank Plaza (445 S. Figueroa Street). (Commercial Observer)

This matters because Downtown L.A. fundamentals remain challenged: the same report cites 31.8% office availability in Downtown L.A. (Savills Q1) and average asking rents around $3.76 per square foot per month. (Commercial Observer)

The deal is not a “market turning” by itself, but it is a useful reminder of where leasing is still possible: well-located towers, competitive economics, and tenants with real operational deadlines.

What it means for CRE: In LA office, transaction volume may stay thin, but motivated tenants can still create marquee comps—especially when landlords price to the market that exists today, not the one from 2019.

2) Rates: The Fed’s “Conditional” Path Keeps Cap Rates Range-Bound

Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a swift end to the war could keep the door open to rate cuts later this year, but he expects near-term inflation pressure from higher energy prices. (Reuters)

That conditionality is the key input for valuation. If energy shocks fade quickly, markets can re-price the forward curve lower; if the conflict drags on, “higher-for-longer” becomes the base case—and refinancing math deteriorates across property types.

A Reuters poll of economists (Apr. 17–21) also pointed to reduced confidence in near-term easing, with a slim majority expecting the fed funds rate to remain in the 3.50%–3.75% range through the end of September and inflation forecasts revised higher versus late March. (Reuters via Investing.com)

What it means for CRE: Underwrite exit cap rates and refinance proceeds with wider cushions. The market is trading on scenarios, not certainty—so deals need to survive both “cuts later” and “no cuts” outcomes.

3) Credit Watch: CMBS Delinquencies Rise Again, Office Still the Weak Link

Trepp reported the CMBS delinquency rate increased 41 basis points to 7.55% in March 2026, reversing February’s decline. (Trepp)

Trepp also noted nearly $5.1 billion of newly delinquent loans in the month and described a “churn” dynamic as loans mature, default, cure, and then re-default—typical of a refinancing market where proceeds often don’t match payoff balances. (Trepp)

Office remains a focal point for lenders and buyers because distress tends to be both idiosyncratic (asset-by-asset) and structural (demand, utilization, tenant flight to quality). Even if the broader rate environment improves, the office capital stack is still being re-written.

What it means for CRE: Expect more “credit selection” and fewer blanket rules. Strong sponsorship, in-place cash flow, and demonstrable tenant retention will matter more than pro forma upside.

4) Development Feasibility: Tariffs Reset the Cost Baseline Higher

Cushman & Wakefield estimates that tariff rates as of April 7, 2026 increase construction materials costs about 6% versus a 2024 baseline, with total project costs rising roughly 3%. (Cushman & Wakefield)

In practical underwriting terms, that 3% can erase a meaningful share of a developer’s margin—especially on deals that were already tight due to high interest carry and slower lease-up assumptions.

The implication is not “no development,” but a narrower development universe: projects with embedded advantages (entitlements in hand, unique locations, pre-leasing, or subsidized capital) will move; marginal deals will stall.

What it means for CRE: For buyers, slower new supply can be supportive over the medium term. For developers, feasibility thresholds are higher—plan on more equity, more time, and more contingency.

1) Rates: The “First Cut” Is Getting Pushed Out — and That Matters More Than the Number

Brokerage economists are increasingly converging on a later starting point for easing, even if they still see cuts in 2026. Reuters reported that Goldman Sachs, BofA Global Research, and Barclays forecast two Fed cuts in 2026, but shifted their expected first cut from June to September. (Reuters)

At the same time, the range of outcomes is widening: Deutsche Bank now expects the Fed to hold rates unchanged through 2026, citing oil-driven inflation risk, resilient growth, and a tight labor market. (Reuters)

What it means for CRE:

  • When the timing of relief pushes out, lenders and buyers tend to underwrite a higher “carry cost” for longer, which increases sensitivity to vacancy, lease rollover, and capex timing.
  • The practical implication for LA is that business plan risk (re-tenanting, redevelopment, heavy capex) is still penalized, while stabilized cash flow remains the most financeable.

2) Energy Shock Is Feeding Inflation Uncertainty — and That Flows Straight into Cap Rates

Oil is acting like the macro “wild card” again. Reuters noted that Goldman Sachs cut its second-quarter 2026 oil price forecasts to Brent $90/bbl and WTI $87/bbl following a U.S.-Iran two-week ceasefire, underscoring just how quickly pricing can swing with geopolitical headlines. (Reuters)

The same Reuters report highlighted ANZ’s warning that supply disruptions can flip the market into deficit, and that prices may need to stay above $100/bbl to manage demand if recovery stalls. (Reuters)

What it means for CRE:

  • Higher and more volatile oil tends to push inflation expectations up, keeping base rates “sticky” and delaying refinance windows.
  • Construction inputs and operating costs can re-accelerate, which shifts value toward assets with (i) near-term rent reset ability and/or (ii) contractual expense pass-throughs.

3) Debt Markets: CMBS Delinquencies Are Still Elevated — and Sector Dispersion Is the Story

What it means for CRE:

  • Capital stacks are being judged by sector and micro-location, not “CRE broadly.” This is why industrial credit remains comparatively resilient while office and certain lodging exposures face tighter terms.
  • For investors, the actionable point is to model refinance outcomes conservatively: higher debt yields, more reserves, and wider spreads for anything with leasing risk or capex needs.

4) LA Office: Selective Demand Is Real — and Big Leases Still Happen When the Use Case Is Mission-Critical

Downtown LA’s vacancy/availability remains high, but tenants with clear operational needs are still committing.   • High availability doesn’t eliminate demand; it changes it. Tenants are trading up into better buildings (or better deals) and using leverage to secure flexibility, TI, and concessions.
  • For owners, the underwriting question is no longer “Will leasing recover?” but “What quality tier are we in, and what is our cost to compete?”

  • Retail (STNL / Neighborhood): Favor necessity-based tenants and leases with clear expense pass-throughs; be cautious on “tourism-forward” revenue assumptions if macro volatility persists.

Fed policy expectations and capital markets liquidity remain the swing factors for Southern California commercial real estate in 2026. The March 17–18 FOMC minutes (released April 8) showed a growing openness to keeping rate hikes on the table if inflation persists, with policymakers highlighting energy-driven inflation risk tied to the U.S.-Israeli conflict with Iran (Reuters). In Los Angeles, a major Olympics-linked Downtown LA office lease is a reminder that specific demand drivers can still cut through weak market-level fundamentals (Commercial Observer).

Below is what changed, what we’re watching next, and how LA investors can underwrite the next 6–18 months.

1) Fed Minutes: “Higher for Longer” Now Includes Hike Optionality

The minutes of the March Fed meeting introduced a more two-sided distribution of outcomes than markets had been pricing: an increasing number of officials saw a scenario where additional tightening could be warranted if inflation stays above target (Reuters). The Fed held the policy rate steady at 3.50%–3.75% in March, but the bigger signal is that the debate is now explicitly about both cuts and hikes depending on how inflation and growth evolve (Reuters).

For CRE, the practical issue is that rate volatility—not just the absolute level of rates—raises required returns, widens credit spreads, and tightens underwriting. When borrowers and lenders believe the terminal path is uncertain, the market often prices loans and cap rates with an added “insurance premium,” pressuring refinance proceeds even if the Fed does not move.

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwrite refinancings with more sensitivity around exit cap rates and spread widening, not just base-rate forecasts, as the Fed discussion expands to include hike optionality.

2) Oil Shock Transmission: Energy Volatility Can Reprice Costs Faster Than Leases Reset

The Fed minutes tied inflation risk to a sustained rise in oil prices and noted that, after several years of above-target inflation, longer-term inflation expectations could become more reactive to energy price swings (Reuters). Reuters reported that oil prices surged over 50% after the Feb. 28 conflict outbreak and were still around $92 per barrel after falling more than 15% following a ceasefire (Reuters).

In CRE underwriting terms, energy is not a “macro headline”—it is a through-line input. It hits operating expenses (utilities), capital projects (materials and shipping), and the construction pipeline (diesel, logistics, petrochemical-based inputs). The key risk is timing mismatch: expenses and capex can rise quickly, while lease roll and rent resets take quarters or years.

What it means for CRE:

  • For value-add and redevelopment, treat energy-driven volatility as a budget and schedule risk: widen contingency and revisit price-lock assumptions when underwriting capex-heavy business plans.

On Location—the official hospitality provider for the 2028 Summer Olympics—signed a 108,272-square-foot lease at Union Bank Plaza (445 S. Figueroa) in Downtown Los Angeles, described as Southern California’s largest office lease so far this year (Commercial Observer). The lease matters less as a broad “DTLA is back” indicator and more as a case study in where office demand still shows up: event-driven operations, mission-critical teams, and groups that value adjacency to transportation and civic nodes.

The market backdrop remains challenging. What it means for CRE:

  • Office underwriting is increasingly “tenant story + building story”; market averages are less predictive when demand concentrates in a few use-cases and a few submarkets.

4) Credit Thermometer: CMBS Delinquencies Rose Again, Led by Lodging and Office

Trepp reported that the CMBS delinquency rate increased by 41 basis points to 7.55% in March 2026, reversing February’s decline (Trepp). At the property-type level, Trepp said lodging posted the largest increase (up 137 bps to 7.31%), while office rose 51 bps and multifamily increased 30 bps (industrial dipped slightly) (Trepp).

This matters for private-market CRE even when you are not directly exposed to securitized loans. CMBS is a real-time “stress indicator” for collateral performance and refinancing friction; rising delinquencies often correlate with tighter debt terms, higher reserves, and more conservative appraisals—especially for office-heavy or capex-heavy properties.

What it means for CRE:

  • Treat CMBS performance as an early warning system: rising delinquency in lodging/office can translate into broader lender conservatism and tighter proceeds, even for stabilized assets.

5) The 2026 Maturity Wall Is Back-Loaded—and Debt Yield Is the Tripwire

Beyond near-term delinquency, Trepp flagged the refinancing calendar risk building through the year. Trepp reported that $76.6 billion in CMBS hard maturities are due in 2026, with 39% falling in Q4 alone (Trepp). Trepp also said 36% of these loans have debt yield at or below 8%—the segment most likely to face refinancing friction—with office, retail, and multifamily carrying the highest concentration of this exposure (Trepp).

Debt yield is an underwriting “truth serum” in this cycle because it forces the question: how much NOI supports the loan balance, independent of cap-rate assumptions. If debt yield is low, a lender can only get comfortable by reducing proceeds, charging more, demanding fresh equity, or adding structural protections like cash management.

What it means for CRE:

  • Expect more equity checks and structured workouts as 2026 maturities accelerate; borrowers with low debt yield face the highest risk of refinancing shortfalls and forced decisions.

What This Means for LA Investors

Watch energy-driven expense volatility and keep OPEX assumptions realistic as inflation risks re-emerge.
  • Industrial: Underwrite rent growth more selectively than 2021–2022, but remember infill remains structurally supply-constrained; maintain conservative exit caps if rate volatility persists.
  • Retail: Favor daily-needs and service-heavy retail where tenants can absorb cost pressure; refinancing friction is likely to be higher for weaker centers as maturities stack up.

Hike Optionality, $92 Oil, and a Back-Loaded Maturity Wall: What the Week’s Data Means for LA CRE

The March FOMC minutes confirmed a genuinely two-sided rate debate, with energy-driven inflation keeping hike optionality alive even as ‘many participants’ still favor cuts. CMBS delinquencies reversed February’s improvement with a 41-bps jump to 7.55%, construction input costs are rising at a 12.6% annualized rate, and a 108,272-SF Olympics-linked DTLA office lease offers the week’s clearest example of where office demand still converts.

Capital markets conditions for Los Angeles commercial real estate continued to evolve in a more uncertain direction this week. The release of the March 17–18 FOMC minutes on April 8 confirmed a more explicitly two-sided Fed—one where cuts remain the base case for many officials but hike optionality is now formally on the table for some, driven specifically by energy-linked inflation risks (Reuters). Meanwhile, Trepp’s full March CMBS delinquency report showed the rate climbing to 7.55%—reversing February’s decline—with lodging and office leading the deterioration (Trepp). Construction costs are accelerating at the fastest annualized pace in recent memory, and the 2026 maturity wall remains a Q4-concentrated risk that will test borrowers with thin debt yields.

On the transaction side, an Olympics-driven 108,272-SF office lease in Downtown LA is the largest Southern California office deal so far in 2026—worth understanding in detail as an example of what still moves in a structurally oversupplied market (Commercial Observer).

Here is a section-by-section breakdown of the week’s key data and what it means for owners, operators, and investors across LA’s property types.

1) Fed Minutes: A Genuinely Two-Sided Rate Debate

The minutes of the March FOMC meeting introduced explicit hike optionality into the Fed’s public posture for the first time in this cycle. “Some participants” saw a “compelling argument” to preserve the ability to raise rates if inflation stays above target; “many participants” still viewed rate cuts as part of the baseline outlook (Reuters). The Fed held the policy rate steady at 3.50%–3.75% in March.

The specific driver cited in the minutes is energy. Officials highlighted the risk that a sustained rise in oil prices—linked to the U.S.-Israeli conflict with Iran—could keep inflation elevated longer than the baseline assumes. The minutes also noted concern that after several years of above-target inflation, long-term inflation expectations could become more sensitive to energy-price swings (Reuters). Oil had surged more than 50% from the February 28 conflict outbreak before falling more than 15% to approximately $92 per barrel following a two-week ceasefire—a $92 floor that is still well above where CRE borrowing cost models were built eighteen months ago.

What it means for CRE:

  • The rate debate is no longer one-directional; underwrite refinancings and hold-period analysis with explicit sensitivity to both a cut scenario and a hike scenario.
  • Rate volatility itself—not just absolute rate levels—raises required returns and widens credit spreads; even a “no move” Fed creates uncertainty premiums in lender pricing.
  • Floating-rate borrowers should test their debt service coverage under a rate increase of 50–100 bps before assuming the next move is down.

2) Oil at $92: Energy-Driven Costs Hit CRE Faster Than Leases Reset

Oil remaining near $92 per barrel—even after a ceasefire-driven pullback from over $100—continues to drive elevated input costs across the real estate operating and capital expenditure stack (Reuters). Construction Dive’s reporting on ABC data showed nonresidential construction input prices up 1.3% month-over-month in February and 3.7% year-over-year, with energy-related inputs leading: natural gas +10.9% month-over-month, unprocessed energy materials +6% month-over-month, crude petroleum +4.7% month-over-month (Construction Dive).

ABC Chief Economist Anirban Basu specifically warned that with oil near $100 per barrel at publication time, higher diesel and shipping costs could push materials prices further and serve as a headwind to construction activity (Construction Dive). The 12.6% annualized input cost increase through February represents the clearest near-term risk to any development pro forma or value-add renovation underwriting completed before the conflict escalation.

What it means for CRE:

  • Re-run capex budgets for any project in pre-construction or early construction; price-lock periods from late 2025 or early 2026 Q1 may no longer reflect current contractor bids.
  • Operational cost assumptions for energy-intensive property types (industrial, large multifamily, hospitality) should be stress-tested against sustained elevated energy input pricing.
  • New development feasibility margins—already compressed by elevated land and financing costs—face additional pressure; expect fewer speculative starts and stronger underlying demand for existing industrial and multifamily product.

3) DTLA’s 2026 Blockbuster Office Lease: What It Says About Where Demand Lives

On Location, the Endeavor-owned official hospitality provider for the 2028 Summer Olympics, signed a 108,272-square-foot lease at Union Bank Plaza (445 S. Figueroa Street) in Downtown Los Angeles (Commercial Observer). The deal is described as Southern California’s largest office lease signed so far in 2026. Union Bank Plaza totals 701,888 square feet, and the new lease represents approximately 15% of the building’s rentable area.

Context matters here: Union Bank Plaza traded roughly 18 months ago at approximately $80 million, or about $114 per square foot (Commercial Observer). That deeply discounted basis gives ownership structural flexibility to undercut market asking rents or offer substantial tenant improvement packages—a competitive advantage that higher-basis buildings cannot match. DTLA availability overall stands at 31.8% versus 27.3% across Greater LA, and average DTLA asking rent is $3.76 per square foot per month versus $4.06 for the broader market (Commercial Observer)—confirming that the submarket is still functionally oversupplied. The Olympics-linked deal is also worth flagging as a data point on Capital Group’s reported interest in nearby Bank of America Plaza at approximately $150 per square foot—a sign that institutional buyers are beginning to re-engage DTLA at selectively low basis points.

What it means for CRE:

  • Office demand in this cycle rewards specificity: the tenants executing leases have defined missions, fixed timelines, and operational reasons to be in a physical location—not merely preference.
  • Buildings acquired at distressed pricing with the flexibility to offer competitive economics remain more relevant in today’s leasing environment than assets burdened by full-cycle cost structures.
  • Watch the Capital Group / Bank of America Plaza transaction for price discovery signals on well-located DTLA office at institutional scale.

4) CMBS Credit Stress: March Reversal Points to Persistent Pressure

Trepp’s full March 2026 CMBS delinquency report showed the rate rising 41 basis points to 7.55%, reversing February’s decline and signaling that conditions have not stabilized (Trepp). Lodging posted the sharpest increase—up 137 basis points to 7.31%, the first time above 7% since April 2025’s peak of 7.85%. Office rose 51 basis points; multifamily climbed 30 basis points; industrial edged down slightly as the lone bright spot (Trepp).

Trepp also reported that nearly $5.1 billion became newly delinquent in March, with approximately 40% of new delinquencies representing previously performing matured balloons—loans that had been current on payments but failed to refinance at maturity (Trepp). This “matured balloon” category is the more instructive signal: it indicates that refinancing friction is translating into technical defaults even for performing assets, not just for operationally distressed properties.

What it means for CRE:

  • Treat the 40% “matured balloon” share of new delinquencies as evidence that refinancing friction—not operational weakness—is the dominant stress driver; this has implications for any asset approaching loan maturity in the next 12 months.
  • Rising delinquency in lodging and office sectors will likely translate into tighter lender posture across the capital stack for those property types within one to two quarters.
  • Industrial’s relative delinquency stability reinforces it as the preferred collateral type for debt capital; the spread between industrial and office debt pricing is likely to widen further.

5) Managing the 2026 Maturity Calendar Before Q4 Crunch

The back-loaded structure of the 2026 CMBS maturity calendar compounds all of the above risks. With approximately 39% of the year’s $76 billion in hard maturities concentrated in Q4, borrowers who delay refinancing conversations are effectively competing for the same lender bandwidth at the same time (Trepp). Loans with debt yield at or below 8%—meaning NOI covers debt service with limited cushion—represent the highest-risk cohort for refinancing shortfalls and forced decisions.

The broader Trepp framework positions 2026 as a “sorting year” where capital availability is a function of asset quality and risk tier rather than a uniform market condition. Stabilized, cash-flowing assets with strong NOI coverage are finding debt capital—sometimes on competitive terms. Transitional, lower-occupancy, or energy-cost-exposed assets are facing more selective access and higher cost, with lenders requiring additional equity, structural protections, or both.

What it means for CRE:

  • Begin lender conversations for any loan maturing in H2 2026 now; earlier engagement provides more time to negotiate, less competition for lender attention, and more options if the first refinancing path fails.
  • Audit portfolio debt yield across all properties; anything below 8% should be treated as a near-term risk requiring a proactive plan.
  • Value-add sponsors with execution timelines extending into 2026 Q4 should plan for the possibility that bridge-to-perm refinancing timelines extend, not compress, as the maturity wall builds.

What This Means for LA Investors

  • Multifamily: Energy-driven expense pressure and rising insurance costs are compressing NOI margins faster than rent growth can fully offset; stress-test operating budgets and resist over-levering against projected rather than in-place income. Loss-to-lease capture and operational efficiency remain the most defensible value-add strategies.
  • Industrial: Southern California infill industrial supply constraints remain a structural tailwind; however, construction cost acceleration raises the development cost floor, which supports existing asset pricing even as exit cap uncertainty persists. Maintain conservative exit cap assumptions.
  • Retail: Daily-needs, grocery-anchored, and service-oriented retail formats continue to demonstrate operational resilience; consumer cost pressure from energy prices tends to shift spending toward necessity and away from discretionary categories. For maturing retail loans, verify debt yield is comfortably above lender minimum thresholds.
  • Office: The On Location / Olympics lease is a real transaction with real implications, but it reflects a specific, time-bound demand driver operating inside a market that remains 31.8% available in DTLA. California's Insurance Crisis Is Hitting Commercial Real Estate Hard — Here's What to Know

Rising premiums, insurer withdrawals, and the exploding FAIR Plan are reshaping the economics of owning commercial property in California. New 2026 legislation offers some relief, but the crisis is far from over.

The Crisis in Numbers

California's property insurance market is under severe stress. Major insurers have pulled back from issuing new policies, premiums are spiking, and the state's insurer of last resort — the California FAIR Plan — has seen its exposure surge 37% year-over-year, with policies more than doubling in wildfire-prone zones over the past four years. According to Bloomberg, 14% of FAIR Plan policies now cover properties in urban zones with low fire risk — a sign that the crisis has spread well beyond wildfire zones.

For commercial property owners, the impact is direct: higher operating costs, more difficult financing, and in some cases, an inability to obtain adequate coverage at any price.

Why This Matters for CRE

Insurance availability is a fundamental requirement for commercial real estate transactions. Without adequate coverage, lenders won't finance, buyers can't close, and property values decline. The Reuters 2026 CRE analysis identified rising insurance costs as a potential "deal-breaker" in certain California markets. And a legal analysis from WSHB warns that the FAIR Plan's commercial coverage cap of $20 million per location is "far below replacement costs" for many larger properties — leaving buildings structurally underinsured.

New 2026 Laws Offer Some Relief

California has enacted several new laws effective January 1, 2026, aimed at stabilizing the market. According to the California Department of Insurance, the key provisions include:

  • Business Insurance Protection Act (SB 547): Extends the existing non-renewal moratorium to commercial policies, covering businesses, HOAs, condominiums, affordable housing, and nonprofits — a significant new protection for commercial property owners.
  • FAIR Plan Stability Act (AB 226): Allows the FAIR Plan to access catastrophe bonds and lines of credit, providing additional financial backing for the state's insurer of last resort.
  • Wildfire safety grants and discounts: New programs incentivize fire-resistant improvements with expanded insurance discounts and faster claim payouts.

Additionally, SB 1076, introduced in February, would require insurers to offer coverage to any property meeting wildfire-safety standards starting in 2028 — a potential game-changer if passed.

What Commercial Property Owners Should Do Now

The insurance landscape is evolving rapidly. For commercial property owners in the LA market, proactive steps include:

  • Review your coverage immediately. Ensure your policy limits reflect current replacement costs, not outdated valuations.
  • Invest in fire-resistant improvements. Properties that meet wildfire-safety standards may qualify for better rates and, potentially, guaranteed coverage under pending legislation.
  • Budget for higher insurance costs. Factor realistic insurance projections into NOI calculations for acquisitions and refinancing.
  • Explore surplus lines and alternative markets. Specialty insurers and excess & surplus lines carriers may offer options when standard markets won't.

LA Commercial Real Estate in Spring 2026: Stabilization, Bifurcation, and Where Capital Is Flowing

As we move into Q2 2026, the commercial real estate landscape in Los Angeles — and nationally — is defined by a striking paradox. Record-high office vacancy exists alongside historic demand for industrial, data center, and healthcare real estate. Here's what investors and property owners need to know.

The Big Picture: Stabilization With Caution

Industry experts are calling 2026 a year of "stabilization and recovery" for commercial real estate. Meanwhile, CoStar reports that sales volume surged more than 40% year-over-year in Q3 2025, and banks are "gradually re-entering the commercial real estate lending space."

But the picture is far from uniform. As Lincoln Property Company notes, "extreme distress exists in some sectors, while demand exists in others." The markets thriving in 2026 are those with structural, long-term demand drivers — population migration, technology infrastructure, and essential services — rather than those dependent on pre-pandemic assumptions.

Los Angeles Office: Record Vacancy, But Signs of Life

Los Angeles County's office sector entered 2026 with record-high vacancy. According to Marcus & Millichap's Q1 2026 report, both CBD and suburban vacancy hover around 20%, with availability above 17% across all six of the metro's largest submarkets. The overall county office vacancy stands at a staggering 23.4%.

However, there are emerging bright spots. Class B/C office space saw positive net absorption during each quarter of 2025, suggesting that smaller tenants and service-based businesses are re-entering the market. California logged a record number of high-propensity business applications last year, and Los Angeles County historically captures the most filings in the state — a leading indicator for future leasing activity.

Industrial: Normalized but Still Resilient

LA County's industrial vacancy has climbed to 4.6% — the highest in a decade — according to Cushman & Wakefield's Q4 2025 SoCal report. But context matters: rents remain roughly 48% above pre-pandemic levels, and net absorption turned positive on an annual basis for the first time since 2022.

South Bay industrial assets — particularly in Torrance, El Segundo, Carson, and Gardena — continue to outperform, driven by e-commerce fulfillment, last-mile logistics, aerospace and defense, and port-related demand.

Multifamily: Rents Stabilizing as Supply Peaks

Multifamily has led CRE investment volume since 2015, and CoStar projects that trend will continue, though its share of total volume may dip as investors allocate more capital toward office, retail, and industrial. Rents are stabilizing as a record wave of new supply enters the market nationally.

Where Capital Is Flowing in 2026

The most significant trend of 2026, according to Lincoln Property Company's analysis, is capital reallocation toward sectors with durable demand. Three areas stand out:

  • Data centers: AI workloads are growing at 33% compound annual rates, overwhelming existing electrical grids and creating urgent demand for specialized real estate.
  • Healthcare/medical office: Outpatient care delivery is a permanent and growing demand driver, with institutional capital flowing into medical office assets.
  • Last-mile logistics: E-commerce continues to drive demand for well-located distribution space near population centers.

"Smile-state" metros like Dallas-Fort Worth, South Florida, and Austin attract disproportionate investment, but coastal markets with structural advantages — including parts of greater Los Angeles — remain competitive for the right asset classes.

Risks on the Horizon

Several headwinds deserve attention:

  • $875 billion in CRE mortgages mature in 2026, creating refinancing pressure, especially for office assets (Commercial Real Estate News, March 2026).

  • Insurance costs are rising sharply due to climate risk, becoming a potential deal-breaker in certain markets (Reuters).

Regulatory Watch

Two regulatory developments highlighted by Reuters' 2026 CRE analysis are particularly relevant for California:

  • Building Performance Standards (BPS): The first compliance cycle ends in 2026, forcing immediate capital investment decisions for energy efficiency upgrades.
  • Transit-oriented housing: California's updated law, taking effect mid-2026, will restrict local zoning limitations near major transit corridors — a catalyst for mixed-use and office-to-residential conversions.

For commercial property owners and investors in the Los Angeles market, the message in Spring 2026 is nuanced. Industrial and well-located office assets with strong amenities are outperforming.
