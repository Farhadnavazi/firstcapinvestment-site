SB 79 Live Wednesday — But LA's Version Is Much Smaller Than the State's

SB 79, the Abundant & Affordable Homes Near Transit Act, takes effect July 1. (NJTOD) The headline state numbers are familiar by now: up to 95 feet of height and 160 dwelling units per acre on parcels within a half-mile of qualifying transit. (Money Talks News) Tiered by-right minimums run 7-9 stories within a quarter-mile of heavy rail, 5-6 stories within a half-mile of major rail, and 4-5 stories within a half-mile of qualifying high-frequency bus service. (Cali ADU)

The LA story is much narrower. On June 17 the City Council voted unanimously (with Traci Park absent) to pair the new Low-Rise Ordinance with a Phased Implementation Ordinance (CF 25-1083-S4) that defers full SB 79 compliance in qualifying areas until one year after the next Housing Element update -- roughly 2030. (LAist) (Cali ADU) The mechanism: SB 79 allows transit-adjacent jurisdictions that already permit at least 50% of the state-required density to opt out temporarily. LA met that threshold by adopting the Low-Rise Ordinance, which permits 2-to-4-story multifamily buildings of 4 to 16 units within a half-mile of 57 designated Opportunity Stations -- including the new D Line extension, parts of the San Fernando Valley near G Line stops, and portions of Eagle Rock along the planned NoHo-to-Pasadena bus rapid transit corridor. (LAist) Both ordinances still need final approval from Mayor Karen Bass.

Pasadena followed suit on June 22, voting 5-0 (with the mayor, vice mayor, and a councilmember recused for nearby property interests) to adopt a partial 18-month exemption from SB 79 for single-family and lower-density multifamily sites in six Metro A Line transit zones. (Pasadena Now) Beverly Hills adopted a Transit-Oriented Development Alternative Plan on June 9 that shifts 50% of mandated density away from single-family neighborhoods into a mixed-use overlay along Wilshire east of La Cienega; SB 79 applications cannot be filed in Beverly Hills until the state approves the city's plan. (Beverly Hills Courier) The practical takeaway for sponsors: if your project is in unincorporated LA County, smaller cities without an approved alternative plan, or Davis Vanguard's estimated million-unit statewide envelope, July 1 changes your underwriting. If you're inside LA, Pasadena, or Beverly Hills, the new opportunity is the Low-Rise Ordinance and TODAP work -- not full SB 79 -- for the next four years. (Davis Vanguard)

DTLA Distress Deepens: 601W Adds Wells Fargo North; BAC Plaza Now the Benchmark at ~$150/SF

The downtown office story added another data point this week. 601W Companies secured a $132 million senior loan from Northwind Group to acquire the 55-story Wells Fargo Center North Tower at 333 South Grand Avenue after Brookfield ran into distress on a $506 million debt against the property. (Commercial Observer) (Yahoo Finance) That's the third major DTLA office trade in a row to clear in the $130-$180-per-square-foot range, after Capital Group's $210M BAC Plaza buy at ~$150/SF and 601W's earlier $180M acquisition of the same Wells Fargo Center's other tower. (The Real Deal) The Real Deal puts the long-arc decline in context: BAC Plaza was worth $605 million ($430/SF) about a decade ago, and the typical pre-pandemic DTLA Class A trade cleared around $450/SF. (The Real Deal) Overall DTLA office vacancy is now near 35%. (The Real Deal) Institutional capital is exiting; opportunistic and owner-user capital is showing up to set the floor.

ACA 22 Replaces the Howard Jarvis Ballot Measure

The California Legislature voted overwhelmingly the week of June 22 to advance ACA 22 -- the legislative version of the Local Taxpayer Protection Act preserving Prop. 13's two-thirds-vote requirement for special taxes -- and to remove ACA 13, the 2023 "poison pill" amendment, from the November ballot. (Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association) In exchange, the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association withdrew its own statewide initiative that would have capped local real-estate transfer taxes at 0.05%. (Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association) ACA 22 will appear on the November ballot with a proposition number to be assigned, but it does not contain the transfer-tax cap. For LA owners weighing whether to push an above-threshold deal to record before the new $5.4M/$10.9M ULA thresholds take effect July 1, the implication is direct: there is no longer a statewide ballot vehicle that would gut ULA after November. The only structural ULA fix on the table is the LA-only multifamily carve-out that City Council voted 9-5 to draft on June 17.

Warsh's First FOMC: A Hold, a Hawkish Dot Plot, and a Statement Stripped of Easing Bias

(Federal Reserve) (Fox Business) The accompanying statement was shorter than prior versions and dropped the easing bias outright. (KPMG) The real story was the Summary of Economic Projections. The median 2026 funds-rate dot moved up from 3.4% in March to 3.8% -- a level that flips the committee's implied next move from a cut to a hike. (Morningstar) Nine of the eighteen participants now project at least one rate increase in 2026. (Morningstar) The Fed also marked up median 2026 core PCE from 2.7% to 3.3% and trimmed 2026 GDP growth to 2.2% from 2.4%. (NDTV Profit) Markets responded immediately: CME FedWatch odds of at least a 25-bp hike before year-end jumped to 36.4%, with 33.7% odds of a 50-bp hike -- up from 14.8% the day before. (Fox Business) Bond-futures markets now imply more than 85% odds of a hike this year, with October on the table. (Morningstar) (CNBC) KPMG now expects two hikes by year-end. LA Council Reverses: A November Ballot Measure to Carve New Multifamily Out of ULA

Just two weeks after the ad hoc committee declined to recommend any structural reform, the full LA City Council voted 9-5 on June 17 to direct the city attorney to draft a ballot measure that would amend Measure ULA by exempting newly built multifamily properties sold within ten years of construction. (Commercial Observer) The draft will return to the Council for final approval before it can be placed on the November ballot. (Commercial Observer) The empirical case driving the pivot is now well documented. RAND found ULA has cut high-value real-estate transactions by 31%, and multifamily and commercial transactions are down more than 46% since the tax took effect in 2023. (Commercial Observer) Production of large multifamily developments has fallen 30%. (Commercial Observer) Developer Geoff Burton told CO bluntly: "We are not underwriting new development deals in Los Angeles. L.A. has become near impossible to build new housing because of ULA." (Commercial Observer) For sponsors holding land or sites in the entitlement pipeline, this is the most actionable LA news of the year so far -- though the carve-out only matters if voters approve it, and the statewide Howard Jarvis Local Taxpayer Protection Act remains on the same November ballot.

ULA Reform Is Dead at City Hall — for Now

The LA City Council's ad hoc committee on Measure ULA, chaired by Council Member Ysabel Jurado, held its final meeting on May 29, 2026 and announced it will not recommend a ballot measure to amend the transfer surcharge. The committee will instead propose only administrative and technical adjustments around appeals processes and hardship provisions. (Reed Corporation) For owners and sponsors who have been waiting on a Council fix before closing an above-threshold LA trade, the message is now explicit: stop waiting. The only path to structural change runs through the November ballot.

That ballot vehicle is the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association's "Local Taxpayer Protection Act," certified by the Secretary of State on May 3, 2026. (Reed Corporation) If voters approve it on November 4, every municipal real-estate transfer tax in California would be capped at 0.05% of gross sale price. On a $10 million LA sale, the city's take would drop from $550,000 today to $5,000 — a reduction of more than 99%. (Reed Corporation)

July 1 Threshold Bump: $5.4M and $10.9M Take Effect

Measure ULA carries an annual inflation adjustment. For transactions recording on or after July 1, 2026, the new thresholds are $5,400,000 (for the 4% rate) and $10,900,000 (for the 5.5% rate) — up from the original $5M and $10M. (Reed Corporation) The recording date governs, not the contract date, so deals in escrow that record before July 1 use the current thresholds; recordings on or after July 1 use the new ones. Sellers pricing in the $5M-$5.4M and $10M-$10.9M bands need to make a decision on whether to push for a pre-July 1 recording or accept the bump.

RAND Quantifies the Damage

(Globe Street) RAND also pegs the apartment-permit drop at roughly 1,900 units per year — 15% to 18% of the total annual permitted units among larger apartment projects. (Globe Street) Since taking effect, ULA has generated $1.16 billion for affordable housing programs, but actual revenue has lagged initial projections and the city now sits under a negative outlook from major credit-rating agencies. Fresh data points from Trepp and MSCI underline a CRE market still governed by expensive capital: CMBS delinquencies remain elevated, office stress is showing up in special servicing, and price recovery is uneven—stronger in secondary markets than in gateway metros like Los Angeles. For LA investors, the next 60–120 days will likely hinge on inflation prints, long-term Treasury yields, and how lenders price refinance risk across office and value-add multifamily.

Read Full Article →

1) The rates backdrop: inflation prints still drive CRE borrowing costs

Trepp’s latest “Weekly Watch” frames the near-term CRE rate question around the April Core PCE inflation release and whether inflation is becoming more “embedded” in the underlying data—because that outcome would keep policy restrictive and extend the window of refinancing pressure into the back half of 2026 (Trepp — Chief Economist’s Weekly Watch).

Even if the Fed funds rate doesn’t move, the market’s price of risk matters most to CRE. Trepp notes long-term Treasury yields have moderated after a recent move higher, but a hotter inflation print could quickly put renewed upward pressure on yields—raising coupons, widening debt-service coverage constraints, and pushing bid-ask spreads wider again (Trepp — Chief Economist’s Weekly Watch).

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwriting should assume rate volatility remains a real risk factor (not a tail risk) through mid-year inflation data.
  • “Good deals” will keep skewing toward sellers who can finance (or accept) today’s cost of capital—rather than waiting for cuts.

2) Pricing is stabilizing—but not evenly, and Los Angeles is still a laggard in the broad index

MSCI pricing data summarized by GlobeSt suggests the recovery is real but geographically uneven: secondary markets posted a 1.8% year-over-year price gain in April, while the six major metros (including Los Angeles) grew only 0.2% (GlobeSt summarizing MSCI).

What’s surprising in the same dataset is the relative strength in office pricing: GlobeSt reports MSCI showed CBD office prices up 4.1% year-over-year (and 0.8% month-over-month), with suburban office up 3.1% year-over-year (GlobeSt summarizing MSCI). That’s not a declaration that “office is back.” It’s a sign that the repricing cycle may be producing a clearing level for specific assets—especially where the basis is already deeply discounted.

What it means for CRE:

  • “Market recovery” headlines can mislead; LA pricing strength can diverge sharply by submarket and asset quality.
  • If office is finding a clearing price, lenders may still be cautious—but equity may be willing to buy at today’s basis.

3) CMBS stress remains elevated; office is still a leading indicator

Trepp reports the CMBS delinquency rate was 7.54% in April 2026—only a marginal improvement from March (7.55%) (Trepp — CMBS Delinquencies). The details matter: Trepp notes the largest newly delinquent loans in April included multiple office loans and multifamily loans (including major coastal markets), plus a national warehouse/distribution portfolio—suggesting stress is not fully contained to a single property type (Trepp — CMBS Delinquencies).

Special servicing data reinforces that office continues to drive “workout volume.” Trepp reports the CMBS special servicing rate increased to 11.38% in March 2026, driven primarily by a heavy concentration of new office loan transfers (Trepp — CMBS 2.0).

What it means for CRE:

  • Office distress remains a macro signal for lender behavior across all asset classes (credit committees tend to generalize risk).
  • Refinance windows are likely to remain tightest where cash flow is most uncertain—value-add office and transitional multifamily.

4) Energy/geopolitics is re-entering the underwriting stack (inflation + capex + tenant risk)

The European Central Bank’s May 2026 Financial Stability Review argues that the outbreak of war in the Middle East has disrupted energy and commodity supply, raised inflation risks, and introduced additional downside risk to growth (European Central Bank — Financial Stability Review). It also highlights disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and attacks on energy infrastructure as drivers of higher oil market volatility (European Central Bank — Financial Stability Review).

For CRE, that combination matters because it can keep rate cuts “pushed out” even if shelter inflation cools. GlobeSt’s MSCI recap explicitly links delayed Fed cuts to energy prices rising on Middle East tensions—keeping deal flow dependent on all-cash buyers or returns compressed by expensive debt (GlobeSt summarizing MSCI).

What it means for CRE:

  • Energy-driven inflation can flow into higher-for-longer rates, higher operating costs, and more conservative lender sizing.
  • Investors should re-check expense assumptions (utilities, insurance, repairs) and tenant sensitivity (especially discretionary retail).

5) A reminder: lenders will still finance quality—especially when construction risk is behind you

Even in a tighter credit environment, lenders will execute for stabilized (or stabilizing) assets with credible sponsorship. Commercial Observer reports Acre provided a $351 million refinance for a 1,572-unit portfolio of eight newly completed suburban multifamily assets across four states, taking out construction debt and providing runway for stabilization (Commercial Observer).

For LA investors, the takeaway isn’t that “money is easy.” It’s that capital is selectively available—and the underwriting conversation looks very different once construction risk is gone and the business plan is clearly about lease-up and operational execution.

What it means for CRE:

  • The bar is higher, but not impossible: clean project narratives and credible stabilization paths still attract financing.
  • Expect more emphasis on DSCR, sponsor liquidity, and “plan B” scenarios (extensions, partial paydowns, pref equity).

What This Means for LA Investors (by property type)

  • Industrial: Prioritize functional infill and well-located logistics with durable tenant demand; underwrite more modest rent growth and keep an eye on broader credit stress signals that can still affect leasing velocity even in “strong” sectors.
  • Multifamily: Assume a longer stabilization timeline for value-add; focus on submarkets with resilient household formation and employer bases; build in higher reserves for turnover and capex.
  • Retail: Underwrite tenant health conservatively; focus on necessity-based and service-oriented centers; stress-test expense lines that can re-accelerate under energy-driven inflation.
  • Office: Treat today’s pricing rebound as “selective clearing,” not a sector-wide recovery; concentrate on well-located, high-quality buildings with a viable repositioning path and realistic lease-up assumptions.

Rates Higher-for-Longer Signals Meet Rising CMBS Special Servicing as SoCal Small-Bay Industrial Stays Tight

Fed minutes and April’s divided FOMC vote reinforce a “higher-for-longer” base case for borrowing costs, even as market pricing still oscillates between a late-cycle hike and a 2027 easing path. At the same time, CMBS stress is migrating: overall delinquency edged down in April, but special servicing climbed on office transfers. In Southern California, capital is still paying up for small-bay industrial with ultra-low vacancy, while construction input prices re-accelerate on oil-driven volatility—complicating underwriting for value-add and ground-up pipelines.

1) The Fed’s message: policy may stay restrictive—and could tighten further

Fed officials are increasingly explicit that if inflation remains “persistently” above target, additional tightening is on the table. The May meeting minutes reported a “significant number” of officials saw rate hikes as potentially necessary if inflation stays elevated, with the target range still at 3.5%–3.75% (CNBC).

That risk is underscored by April’s FOMC vote dynamics. The Fed held rates at 3.5%–3.75% with four dissenters—the most divided vote since 1992—while markets simultaneously priced out near-term cuts and assigned non-trivial odds to a hike over the coming year (Reuters). For CRE, the practical point is not whether the next move is up or down; it’s that the “neutral” baseline for underwriting is meaningfully higher than the 2010s.

What it means for CRE: Borrower sensitivity to 25–50 bps changes is now amplified by tighter DSCRs, lower proceeds, and reduced refinance optionality; deals that work only with a rapid cut cycle should be treated as speculative.

2) CRE lending conditions: competition is in spreads, not structure

Bank lending conditions are not loosening in a way that materially improves proceeds for most CRE loans. Trepp’s read of the April Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS) is that CRE lending standards were “broadly unchanged”—a sign standards remain tight—while lenders compete in spreads rather than relaxing loan-to-value (LTV) or debt-yield guardrails (Trepp).

In other words, the market is fighting over a smaller pool of “bankable” transactions rather than expanding what qualifies. This tends to concentrate liquidity into stabilized assets (and sponsors) that already pencil, leaving transitional business plans more dependent on private credit, preferred equity, or rescue capital.

What it means for CRE: Expect the gap between “financeable at a bank” and “financeable somewhere” to persist—keep capital stacks flexible and treat lender proceeds as a constraint to be optimized, not a given.

3) CMBS distress: delinquencies stabilize, but special servicing rises on office transfers

Headline delinquency can look calm while stress builds in servicing pipelines. Trepp reported the CMBS delinquency rate decreased one basis point to 7.54% in April 2026, but noted a meaningful share of newly delinquent balance tied to large office and multifamily loans (Trepp).

More importantly for price discovery, Trepp reported the overall CMBS special servicing rate rose to 11.38% (up 38 bps), “mainly driven by office” transfers (Trepp). Special servicing is where extensions, modifications, and restructurings happen—often setting the “real” clearing level for troubled collateral.

What it means for CRE: Watch special servicing (not just delinquency) as the leading indicator for motivated sales, appraisal reductions, and the next leg of repricing—especially in office-adjacent collateral pools.

4) Southern California snapshot: small-bay industrial pricing holds as vacancy stays ultra-low

Despite macro uncertainty, demand for small-bay industrial in Southern California remains resilient, and buyers are still underwriting scarcity value. Commercial Observer reported Harbor Associates and Farallon Capital acquired the 280,000 SF Yorba Linda Commerce Center in Orange County for $81M (about $289/SF), with the asset 98% leased at closing (Commercial Observer).

The same report highlights just how tight the shallow-bay submarket is: Orange County vacancy for spaces under 24,000 SF is about 2%, and near 1% for spaces under 10,000 SF—versus 5.7% for larger industrial space (Commercial Observer). That bifurcation matters for LA/OC investors: shallow-bay is behaving like a necessity-based product with stickier tenancy and less new competing supply.

What it means for CRE: Infill shallow-bay remains one of the clearest “durable demand” stories locally, but pricing strength means returns increasingly depend on operational lift (rollover strategy, tenant mix, expense control) rather than cap-rate compression.

5) Construction costs: oil-driven volatility is back in the input stack

Construction underwriting is seeing renewed volatility—bad timing for projects needing refinancing or recapitalization. Construction Executive reported construction input prices “surged again” in April and are up 7.0% year-over-year, with input prices rising 6.2% in the first four months of 2026 versus 4.8% over the prior three years (Construction Executive).

The same update ties escalation to higher oil prices and “tariff-affected materials like iron and steel,” and flags geopolitical uncertainty around the Strait of Hormuz as a continued risk to input price inflation (Construction Executive). When combined with elevated borrowing costs, cost overruns can quickly become capital-structure problems.

What it means for CRE: Re-price contingency and procurement timelines; for deals with construction exposure, treat “basis risk” (cost + rate) as a first-class underwriting variable, not a footnote.

What This Means for LA Investors (by property type)

  • Multifamily: Prioritize deals with clear path to durable NOI growth (rent lifts via renovations, operational efficiency) rather than “rate relief” assumptions; monitor CMBS stress signals for discounted acquisitions tied to refinancing pressure (Trepp).
  • Industrial: Infill/small-bay demand remains strong; underwrite conservative exit caps and focus on lease-roll strategy, since pricing is already reflecting scarcity (Commercial Observer).
  • Retail: Favor necessity-based and service-heavy centers where tenants can pass through inflation; higher-for-longer rates keep tenant credit and lease structure (term, options, bumps) critical in valuation (CNBC).
  • Office: Treat special servicing trends as a leading signal for price resets; focus on irreplaceable locations or conversion angles where the basis is defensible (Trepp).
  • Development / Value-Add: Underwrite wider contingencies and longer schedules; input-cost volatility and financing costs can erase thin-margin deals quickly (Construction Executive).

LA CRE Pulse: SoCal Owner-Users Step In as CMBS Stress Lingers, Fed Cuts Get Pushed Out, and Construction Costs Re-Heat

Southern California’s deal flow is showing a clear theme: more businesses are buying the buildings they occupy as pricing resets and lease economics stay challenging. At the same time, national credit signals remain mixed—Trepp data shows CMBS delinquencies are still elevated, while multifamily risk is increasingly about refinancing math (not occupancy) in private-label CMBS. Add hotter inflation prints and a growing camp expecting fewer (or no) Fed cuts in 2026, and investors are back to underwriting “higher for longer” as a base case—especially with construction input costs rising again on energy and tariff-sensitive materials.

Read Full Article →

1) Southern California’s “owner-user” bid is getting louder (and it’s most visible in LA)

More Southern California companies are shifting from leasing to buying, a trend the Los Angeles Times frames as a signal that the market may be stabilizing as pricing adjusts and high-quality assets reprice to clearer clearing levels (Los Angeles Times).

The same report highlights several transactions that fit the theme: Capital Group’s purchase of Bank of America Plaza in Downtown Los Angeles for roughly $210 million (about $150/SF) from Brookfield, and the State Compensation Insurance Fund’s acquisition of a 158,785-SF Class A office building at 35 N. Lake Ave. in Pasadena for a future regional hub (Los Angeles Times).

Importantly, the “owner-user” story is happening against a still-choppy office backdrop: Los Angeles County office vacancy was cited at 23.6% with more than 1 million square feet returned to the market by tenants contracting footprints (Los Angeles Times).

What it means for CRE: Owner-user buyers can become a stabilizing marginal bid in office and industrial when traditional institutional capital is cautious, but they are also highly rate-sensitive—so underwriting needs to assume slower velocity if borrowing costs stay elevated.

2) Industrial: rents are still high vs. 2019, but vacancy and absorption are doing the talking

Southern California industrial remains structurally important, but the data is now more bifurcated by submarket. In Los Angeles County, leasing activity was cited at 8.7 million square feet in Q1 (strongest opening quarter since 2021) with an average asking lease rate of $1.32/SF/month—still 47% above end-2019—yet rents have declined slowly for 10 consecutive quarters after the pandemic-era surge (Los Angeles Times).

In the Inland Empire, the same report points to a reset: vacancy cited at 8.5% (50 bps QoQ) with net absorption at negative 3.4 million square feet, driven by several very large buildings going dark (Los Angeles Times).

One constructive counterpoint is the pullback in new supply. The Inland Empire was cited as having just 3.3 million square feet under construction (vs. a 40.6 million square foot peak in 2022), suggesting the market is actively working through excess space and may find its footing sooner if demand normalizes (Los Angeles Times).

What it means for CRE: For industrial, the key question is less “what’s the headline rent?” and more “how long does it take to clear large-box vacancy without a new wave of deliveries?”—which directly affects how aggressively investors can underwrite rent growth and exit cap rates.

3) CMBS stress is still elevated—and it’s not just an office story

Trepp reports the CMBS delinquency rate edged down by 1 basis point to 7.54% in April 2026, but the level remains high by pre-2020 standards (Trepp).

Trepp also notes roughly $2.63 billion became newly delinquent in April, with the five largest newly delinquent loans (just over $1.26 billion) spanning multiple property types—office loans in Houston and New York City, multifamily loans in San Francisco and New York City, and a national warehouse/distribution portfolio (Trepp).

That mix matters for investors because it suggests distress is increasingly about financing structure and maturity timing—not only “weak” property types. Even within industrial and multifamily, loans can tip into trouble when the refinance math breaks.

What it means for CRE: Expect lenders and buyers to keep demanding wider spreads, stronger debt yields, and more conservative proceeds for any asset with near-term maturity exposure—particularly when valuations are being re-set off higher cap rates.

4) Multifamily credit risk: occupancy is fine, but the refinance math is the risk factor in CMBS

In a May 21 analysis, Trepp argues that the widening performance gap between agency multifamily and private-label CMBS is driven primarily by structural factors (leverage, debt yield, maturity profile) rather than meaningfully different occupancy outcomes (Trepp).

Trepp highlights that private-label CMBS has more exposure to the high-leverage tail (loans above 80% LTV) and concentrates more heavily in the 7–9% debt-yield range—levels that can become binding in a higher-rate refinance environment without NOI growth or paydown (Trepp).

Crucially, Trepp notes that nearly half of CMBS multifamily balances mature within three years, creating a “front-loaded” execution risk window where a borrower can have solid operations but still face maturity stress (Trepp).

What it means for CRE: For multifamily acquisitions, the underwriting edge is increasingly in capital structure: fixed-rate duration, realistic refinance proceeds, and conservative NOI assumptions matter as much as physical occupancy.

5) Rates and costs: hotter inflation prints push “higher for longer,” while construction inputs rise again

Altus Group summarizes April inflation data as re-accelerating: CPI was reported at 3.8% YoY (headline) with core CPI at 2.8% YoY, and PPI showed a 1.4% month-over-month jump with 6.0% YoY final demand inflation—conditions that make it harder for markets to price near-term easing (Altus Group).

Separately, Reuters reports Nomura now expects the Fed to leave interest rates unchanged in 2026, citing higher inflation and waning support for easing, and notes Nomura’s view that incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh may not have majority support for rate cuts (Reuters via WKZO).

On the real-economy side, construction costs are heating back up. Construction Executive reports construction input prices were up 7.0% year-over-year and up 6.2% in the first four months of 2026, with much of the increase tied to higher oil prices and tariff-affected materials like iron and steel (Construction Executive).

What it means for CRE: If rates stay higher and construction inputs rise, new development feasibility remains constrained—supportive for well-located existing assets, but challenging for heavy value-add plans that depend on predictable renovation budgets.

What This Means for LA Investors (by property type)

  • Multifamily: Focus on durable submarkets and conservative refinance assumptions; pay close attention to debt yield, maturity timing, and realistic exit cap rates—especially when the “credit risk” is mostly capital-structure-driven in CMBS-style underwriting (Trepp).
  • Industrial: Underwrite absorption and lease-up time more than headline rent; Inland Empire vacancy and negative absorption suggest a near-term reset, but reduced construction pipelines can shorten the downcycle if demand stabilizes (Los Angeles Times).
  • Retail: Higher-for-longer rates and sticky inflation keep consumer sensitivity elevated; prioritize necessity-based tenancy, strong trade areas, and cautious leverage while markets price the cost of capital (Altus Group).
  • Office: The owner-user trend can create liquidity for certain well-located assets, but vacancy remains high in LA County; focus on basis, tenant-credit durability, and realistic re-tenanting timelines (Los Angeles Times).

Fed Minutes Flag Upside Inflation Risk as CMBS Office Stress Deepens and Retail Supply Stays Thin

This week’s FOMC minutes reinforced a ‘higher-for-longer’ bias as energy-driven inflation risks and higher Treasury term premiums keep financing costs elevated. At the same time, CMBS data show office-driven stress in special servicing and steep value resets for distressed collateral, while retail fundamentals remain supported by a thin new-supply pipeline and construction-cost math that discourages speculative development.

The Big Picture (Why This Matters Now)

The Federal Reserve’s latest meeting minutes underscore a familiar reality for commercial real estate: even if short-term rates are held steady, inflation and geopolitical shocks can keep longer-term yields—and therefore CRE debt costs—uncomfortably high. According to the minutes, the FOMC maintained the federal funds target range at 3-1/2% to 3-3/4% and emphasized it was “well positioned” to adjust policy based on incoming data and risks (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes).

At the same time, distress signals in securitized CRE finance remain concentrated in office and are spreading in the margins to other property types. Trepp reported April’s CMBS special servicing rate rose to 11.38%, with office transfers the main driver (Trepp).

This post breaks down what changed in rates, credit, and distress—then translates it into a practical playbook for Los Angeles investors.

1) Fed Minutes: Policy On Hold, But Inflation Risks Tilt Higher

The FOMC kept the benchmark rate unchanged (3-1/2% to 3-3/4%), reinforcing that the near-term baseline is “wait and watch,” not imminent easing (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes). The minutes also describe inflation as “elevated” and having moved higher, with a notable role for rising energy prices (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes).

From a CRE perspective, the nuance is that the Fed can hold short rates steady while the market reprices the path of inflation and risk. The minutes note Treasury yields moved higher, and that the 10-year’s move included higher risk premiums (term premium) rather than just inflation expectations (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes). For cap rates and refinance underwriting, that is a key distinction: term premium can be sticky even if inflation prints cool.

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwrite to a “higher-for-longer” debt-cost band, even if the Fed stays on hold.
  • Expect more dispersion in cap rates by asset quality and lease durability as risk premiums rise.
  • Deals that only pencil with aggressive refinance assumptions remain vulnerable.

2) Credit Conditions: Mixed Signals, With CMBS Still a Soft Spot

The minutes point to bank lending standards that are not uniformly tightening. In the April Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS), banks reported further easing in standards in Q1, and staff assessed overall standards around the median since 2011 (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes).

But “not tightening” does not mean “easy.” The same minutes flag credit remaining somewhat tight for small businesses and lower-credit-score mortgage borrowers, and they note CMBS performance as “somewhat weak” (Federal Reserve FOMC minutes). In practice, this looks like selective liquidity: strong sponsorship and clean cash flow still get done, while transitional or vacancy-risk deals face wider spreads, lower leverage, and more structure.

What it means for CRE:

  • Expect bifurcation: stabilized cash-flow assets remain financeable; transitional deals need more equity.
  • CMBS weakness matters for LA office and certain value-add multifamily refinances where securitization execution is part of the capital stack.

3) CMBS Special Servicing: Office Leads the Stress, but Watch Multifamily Margins

Trepp reported the overall CMBS special servicing rate rose to 11.38% (up 38 bps), driven by a heavy concentration of new office loan transfers (Trepp). By property type, office increased 93 bps; multifamily rose 33 bps; industrial rose 20 bps (Trepp).

The takeaway is not that every property type is “in trouble”—it’s that refinancing pressure is showing up first where NOI durability is most questioned (office), and then where expense growth and rent-growth normalization can compress DSCR (some multifamily) as debt resets.

What it means for CRE:

  • Office remains the epicenter of securitized distress; lenders will continue to demand higher reserves and stronger leasing visibility.
  • Multifamily stress is more deal-specific (basis + debt reset), but it can create local buying opportunities where operations can be improved.

4) The Value Reset: Distressed Collateral Is Clearing at Deep Discounts

Trepp’s reappraisal analysis highlights how severe the repricing can be once a loan enters a true workout. In its 2025 CMBS reappraisal cohort, Trepp reported $23B of collateral cleared at a median 53% discount to origination, with distress heavily concentrated in office, retail, and mixed-use (Trepp). Trepp also notes office represented more than half of the reappraised balance (Trepp).

For investors, the lesson is that “extend and pretend” still exists—but when that breaks, value can gap down fast. That can become an acquisition window, but only if the buyer underwrites a realistic lease-up timeline and an exit cap that reflects the new risk premium regime.

What it means for CRE:

  • Distressed pricing is not theoretical; it is being realized in securitized workouts.
  • LA buyers should track maturity walls and special servicing transfers as a forward indicator of forced sellers.

5) Retail: Supply Is Thin, and Construction-Cost Math Is the Gatekeeper

On the fundamentals side, retail continues to be supported by limited new supply. Connect CRE, citing CoStar, reports U.S. retail availability remains about 15% below its prior 10-year average and that the new-supply pipeline is very thin (Connect CRE).

The key constraint is replacement cost. The same report notes construction costs in many markets imply developers need blended rents north of $30–$35/SF, while national blended rents sit in the mid-$20s/SF (Connect CRE). That gap can keep well-located existing centers in a favorable competitive position—especially in infill LA where entitlement friction and land costs already limit new development.

What it means for CRE:

  • Infill, necessity-based retail in LA can remain resilient when new competition is structurally constrained.
  • Replacement-cost discipline supports pricing power for quality operators and well-merchandised centers.

6) Geopolitics and Oil: The “Second-Order” Channel Into Cap Rates

Energy shocks matter to CRE less through operating expenses and more through the interest-rate channel. Recent coverage has emphasized how Middle East supply disruption risk and low inventories can keep oil prices elevated, feeding inflation sensitivity and rate expectations (CNBC).

When oil spikes, markets often price a longer window of restrictive policy (or at least delayed easing). That shows up quickly in longer-term yields and credit spreads—the two inputs that most directly pressure cap rates and refinance proceeds.

What it means for CRE:

  • Use conservative interest-rate scenarios when underwriting acquisitions and refinances.
  • Favor assets where NOI growth is contractual (longer leases) or where mark-to-market upside is near-term and realistic.

What This Means for LA Investors (Actionable Takeaways)

  • Underwrite expense growth and insurance hard; assume slower rent growth vs. 2021–2022.
  • Look for operational upside (unit turns, RUBS, parking, laundry) that is less rate-dependent.

Industrial (Infill / Logistics):

  • Stay focused on functional, power-capable buildings; tenant demand is increasingly tied to automation and higher power needs (a theme echoed in recent industrial coverage) (Bisnow).
  • Underwrite realistic re-leasing assumptions—don’t pay peak rents for peak conditions.

Retail (Neighborhood / Necessity-based):

  • Replacement-cost constraints and limited new supply can support rent resilience in infill LA (Connect CRE).

Office (Select submarkets / repositioning):

The Week in One View

Market signals in mid-May point to a familiar 2026 mix for Los Angeles investors: rate volatility is still the gating factor for pricing, CMBS distress remains elevated but is changing shape (with multifamily delinquencies rising even as office stays the weakest major property type), and local deal flow continues to reward well-capitalized buyers who can underwrite operating-cost risk and tenant demand by submarket.

1) Rates: A High-but-Stable Baseline Keeps Cap-Rate Discovery Slow

The Federal Reserve’s daily rate series shows the effective federal funds rate at 3.63% (May 15, 2026) and the 10-year Treasury at 4.59% (May 15, 2026), which reinforces that borrowing costs remain restrictive even without a fresh policy shock this week. (Federal Reserve H.15)

For Los Angeles deal underwriting, this matters less as a headline rate story and more as a “volatility story”: when the 10-year sits in the mid-4% range, lenders can quote, but spreads and required debt yields still widen quickly for assets with leasing, rollover, or expense uncertainty. That typically slows price discovery—especially for office and value-add multifamily—because the bid-ask gap can’t close until either NOI becomes more predictable or sellers accept the new cost of capital.

What it means for CRE: Expect continued emphasis on structured capital (rate buydowns, partial recaps, preferred equity) and conservative exit caps, with underwriting focused on downside protection rather than pro forma upside.

2) CMBS: Overall Delinquency Dipped, but Multifamily Stress Rose Again

Trepp reports the overall CMBS delinquency rate decreased by 1 basis point to 7.54% in April 2026. (Trepp)

The headline is “slightly better,” but the composition is the real signal for investors. Office remains the most impaired major property type at 11.69% delinquent, even though it ticked down 2 basis points month over month. (Trepp)

Meanwhile, multifamily delinquencies rose to 7.71% (+56 bps) and industrial rose to 0.96% (+31 bps), reminding the market that higher rates and operating-cost pressure can stress even historically resilient sectors if capital structures were set in a different rate regime. (Trepp)

The updated ARO is a significant expansion of the original 1999 ordinance that helped spark downtown LA's residential revival. According to an analysis by JD Supra, the key changes include:

LA Commercial Real Estate in Spring 2026: Stabilization, Bifurcation, and Where Capital Is Flowing

As we move into Q2 2026, the commercial real estate landscape in Los Angeles — and nationally — is defined by a striking paradox. Record-high office vacancy exists alongside historic demand for industrial, data center, and healthcare real estate. Here's what investors and property owners need to know.

The Big Picture: Stabilization With Caution

Industry experts are calling 2026 a year of "stabilization and recovery" for commercial real estate. According to CNBC's 2026 outlook, citing research from Cushman & Wakefield and CoStar, the U.S. real estate market is entering the year with "renewed energy, clearer visibility, and increasing optimism in both leasing and capital markets."

A Deloitte survey of 850 global CRE executives found that 83% anticipate revenue growth by year-end 2026, though that's down slightly from 88% last year. Meanwhile, CoStar reports that sales volume surged more than 40% year-over-year in Q3 2025, and banks are "gradually re-entering the commercial real estate lending space."

But the picture is far from uniform. As Lincoln Property Company notes, "extreme distress exists in some sectors, while demand exists in others." The markets thriving in 2026 are those with structural, long-term demand drivers — population migration, technology infrastructure, and essential services — rather than those dependent on pre-pandemic assumptions.

Los Angeles Office: Record Vacancy, But Signs of Life

Los Angeles County's office sector entered 2026 with record-high vacancy. According to Marcus & Millichap's Q1 2026 report, both CBD and suburban vacancy hover around 20%, with availability above 17% across all six of the metro's largest submarkets. The overall county office vacancy stands at a staggering 23.4%.

However, there are emerging bright spots. Class B/C office space saw positive net absorption during each quarter of 2025, suggesting that smaller tenants and service-based businesses are re-entering the market. California logged a record number of high-propensity business applications last year, and Los Angeles County historically captures the most filings in the state — a leading indicator for future leasing activity.

Industrial: Normalized but Still Resilient

LA County's industrial vacancy has climbed to 4.6% — the highest in a decade — according to Cushman & Wakefield's Q4 2025 SoCal report. But context matters: rents remain roughly 48% above pre-pandemic levels, and net absorption turned positive on an annual basis for the first time since 2022.

South Bay industrial assets — particularly in Torrance, El Segundo, Carson, and Gardena — continue to outperform, driven by e-commerce fulfillment, last-mile logistics, aerospace and defense, and port-related demand.

Multifamily: Rents Stabilizing as Supply Peaks

Multifamily has led CRE investment volume since 2015, and CoStar projects that trend will continue, though its share of total volume may dip as investors allocate more capital toward office, retail, and industrial. Rents are stabilizing as a record wave of new supply enters the market nationally.

Where Capital Is Flowing in 2026

The most significant trend of 2026, according to Lincoln Property Company's analysis, is capital reallocation toward sectors with durable demand. Three areas stand out:

  • Data centers: AI workloads are growing at 33% compound annual rates, overwhelming existing electrical grids and creating urgent demand for specialized real estate.
  • Healthcare/medical office: Outpatient care delivery is a permanent and growing demand driver, with institutional capital flowing into medical office assets.
  • Last-mile logistics: E-commerce continues to drive demand for well-located distribution space near population centers.

"Smile-state" metros like Dallas-Fort Worth, South Florida, and Austin attract disproportionate investment, but coastal markets with structural advantages — including parts of greater Los Angeles — remain competitive for the right asset classes.

Risks on the Horizon

Several headwinds deserve attention:

  • $875 billion in CRE mortgages mature in 2026, creating refinancing pressure, especially for office assets (Commercial Real Estate News, March 2026).
  • California's population challenges persist — over 229,000 residents left for other states in the past year, while immigration into the state fell 65% (DnG Commercial).
  • Tariffs are stalling over one-third of California CRE projects, per the Allen Matkins/UCLA Anderson Forecast survey.
  • Insurance costs are rising sharply due to climate risk, becoming a potential deal-breaker in certain markets (Reuters).

Regulatory Watch

Two regulatory developments highlighted by Reuters' 2026 CRE analysis are particularly relevant for California:

  • Building Performance Standards (BPS): The first compliance cycle ends in 2026, forcing immediate capital investment decisions for energy efficiency upgrades.
  • Transit-oriented housing: California's updated law, taking effect mid-2026, will restrict local zoning limitations near major transit corridors — a catalyst for mixed-use and office-to-residential conversions.

What This Means for LA Investors

For commercial property owners and investors in the Los Angeles market, the message in Spring 2026 is nuanced. Industrial and well-located office assets with strong amenities are outperforming. Multifamily remains a volume leader. And the flight to quality — where tenants choose premium, well-amenitized spaces — continues to widen the gap between Class A and commodity assets.
