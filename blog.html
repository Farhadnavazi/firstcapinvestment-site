Market Update Los Angeles Development Capital Markets Zoning

Billion-Dollar Bets and Zoning Shifts: One Beverly Hills Secures $4.3B, LA Council Greenlights New Density, and Tariffs Cloud the Construction Outlook

A landmark $4.3 billion financing closes for One Beverly Hills, LA's City Council votes to upzone 55 neighborhoods to delay SB 79, a nonprofit signs the county's largest office lease of 2026, and tariff-driven construction cost increases are reshaping deal math across Southern California.

Read Full Article →

One Beverly Hills Closes $4.3 Billion Financing Package

The developers of One Beverly Hills announced last week that they have secured $4.3 billion in financing to complete what is one of the largest real estate projects currently underway in the United States. The financing, composed of a $2.8 billion senior loan led by JPMorgan Chase and a $1.5 billion mezzanine loan from VICI Properties, is among the largest construction financings in the past decade. (Los Angeles Times)

The 17.5-acre mixed-use development at Wilshire and Santa Monica boulevards will feature Beverly Hills' tallest towers, an Aman luxury hotel (the brand's first West Coast property), fewer than 200 ultra-luxury condominiums ranging from 2,550-square-foot two-bedrooms to 25,000-square-foot penthouses, and 8.5 acres of botanical gardens irrigated entirely by recycled water. Phased delivery is scheduled to begin in 2028, with the project expected to generate approximately $40 billion in local economic activity over 30 years. (Business Wire)

For LA investors, the sheer scale of this commitment from institutional capital signals continued confidence in Southern California's luxury and hospitality sectors, even amid broader market uncertainty.

LA City Council Upzones 55 Neighborhoods to Delay SB 79

On March 25, the Los Angeles City Council voted 13-0 to adopt a strategy that would allow four-story, 4-to-16-unit apartment buildings in 55 single-family and low-density areas citywide, primarily in Central LA, West LA, the Eastside, and the San Fernando Valley. The move is designed to delay full implementation of Senate Bill 79, the state law that would otherwise allow buildings up to nine stories near transit stops starting July 1. (Los Angeles Times)

A provision in SB 79 allows cities to push back the law's broadest upzoning effects until 2030 if they voluntarily add density. The council's plan expands the Corridor Transition Program under the Citywide Housing Incentive Program. However, as Councilmember Katy Yaroslavsky noted, no applications were submitted under the existing program in its first year because "the math doesn't work." She introduced a follow-up motion to increase allowable floor areas and update rules so that "homes actually get built." (LAist)

For multifamily developers and investors, this creates both opportunity and uncertainty: new zoning capacity is being added on paper, but practical barriers to development remain. Watch for the follow-up incentive changes that will determine whether this density actually gets built.

Nonprofit Signs LA County's Largest Office Lease of 2026

In a bright spot for the still-struggling LA office market, the North Los Angeles County Regional Center renewed and expanded its headquarters lease at 9200 Oakdale Avenue in Chatsworth, growing its footprint from 112,115 square feet to approximately 166,870 square feet. The deal, brokered by Newmark, is the largest office lease by square footage in LA County year to date and the fourth-largest in the past two years. (The Real Deal)

Context matters: office vacancy in Greater Los Angeles reached 23.4% at the end of 2025, and total leasing volume fell 7.5% year-over-year to 9.6 million square feet, the first time since 2020 that activity failed to surpass the 10-million-square-foot mark. Renewal activity rose 13.7% in Q4, while direct (new) leasing declined 35.6%. The market continues to be driven by renewals and expansions rather than fresh demand. (The Real Deal)

Hollywood Tower Approved, $15B K Line Extension Advances

The LA City Council also upheld approvals for a major mixed-use development at 6000 Hollywood Boulevard, where the Toyota of Hollywood dealership will be replaced by a 35-story residential tower, a six-story office building, townhomes, and ground-floor retail totaling 501,000 square feet with 350 apartments (44 affordable). (Urbanize LA)

Meanwhile, Metro's board of directors voted to approve the San Vicente-Fairfax alignment for the K Line extension, a $15 billion project that would connect Hollywood to the South Bay via West Hollywood. The City of West Hollywood has pledged to raise billions through an Enhanced Infrastructure Financing District to potentially accelerate a construction timeline originally slated for 2041-2047. (Los Angeles Times)

Major transit expansions like this tend to reshape property values along their corridors years before construction begins. Investors with a long-term horizon should be studying parcels along the proposed alignment.

Tariffs Are Reshaping CRE Construction Economics

Tariff-driven cost pressures continue to weigh on new development. According to JPMorgan, the estimated average tariff rate on all imports now stands at 12%, with building materials hit particularly hard: aluminum, copper, and steel parts face tariffs up to 50%, and kitchen cabinets and vanities from some countries carry duties of 25%. The Federal Reserve's Beige Book noted that these pressures, combined with policy uncertainty, have led tenants and investors to delay leasing and investment decisions. (J.P. Morgan)

Southern California's industrial sector has been especially impacted: JPMorgan reports that West Coast warehouse and distribution properties "shed millions of square feet in 2025," with much of the decline centered around Southern California's Asian trading hub. Fed Chair Jerome Powell has characterized the tariff effects as a "one-time price hike" rather than systemic inflation, but markets continue to price in at most one rate cut for 2026 amid persistent uncertainty. (J.P. Morgan)

Capital Markets Snapshot: March 30, 2026

As of March 30, conventional commercial mortgage rates range from 4.99% to 8.75%, with SBA 504 loans at 5.61%-5.79% and CMBS at 5.63%-7.56%. The 10-year Treasury sits at 4.06%, Prime at 6.75%, and 30-day SOFR at 3.66%. Bridge lending ranges from 5.75% to 12.75%. (Commercial Loan Direct)

NAR's March 2026 report noted that inflation held at 2.4% but shelter costs continue to drive pressure, and the Fed has kept policy unchanged while long-term yields remain above 4%, keeping financial conditions relatively tight. Retail remains the tightest CRE sector nationally, while industrial continues to normalize after its pandemic-era peak, and office is gradually stabilizing with Class A leading leasing activity. (NAR)

What It Means for LA Investors

This week's developments underscore a market defined by divergence. At the top, institutional capital is placing enormous bets on LA's luxury segment (One Beverly Hills). In the middle, the office market is stabilizing through renewals rather than growth, and new zoning capacity is being added but may not translate to actual construction without further incentive adjustments. And across every asset class, tariff-inflated construction costs and elevated financing rates are compressing development margins and slowing new starts.

For investors and property owners in the LA market, the playbook remains: underwrite conservatively on financing costs, watch the zoning and transit developments that will reshape neighborhoods over the next decade, and look for value where the market has already repriced. Fed Holds at 3.50%–3.75% as Markets Price a Higher-for-Longer Path; Pasadena Office Sale Highlights Value Reset

The Fed held rates at 3.50%–3.75% but lifted its inflation outlook as oil prices surged, while markets began pricing non-trivial odds of a 2026 rate hike. In CRE, Trepp data show distress is being managed through extensions (not refinancing), and a Pasadena office trade at a steep discount underscores the ongoing value reset in Southern California.

Read Full Article →

Executive Summary

The Federal Reserve kept its benchmark rate steady at 3.50%–3.75% but raised its inflation projections as energy-driven inflation risks intensified. (Reuters)

At the same time, rate futures began pricing a meaningful chance of a 2026 hike—an important signal that the market is shifting from "when do cuts start?" to "how sticky is inflation going to be?" (Reuters)

In credit, the latest CMBS delinquency improvement appears driven more by modifications and extensions than by clean take-outs—suggesting lenders and borrowers are still buying time. (Trepp)

On the ground in Southern California, the Pasadena office sale of Western Asset Plaza at a large discount to prior pricing is another datapoint that "price discovery" in office is still underway. (Commercial Observer)

1) The Fed Held Rates—But the Inflation Path Moved Higher

The Fed held the policy rate at 3.50%–3.75% (11–1 vote), but its projections shifted in a way CRE investors should not ignore. (Reuters)

Reuters reported the Fed raised its end-2026 PCE inflation projection to 2.7% (from 2.4% in December), reflecting higher energy prices and stickier inflation dynamics. (Reuters)

If inflation expectations re-anchor higher, cap-rate compression becomes harder to justify without materially stronger NOI growth—especially for stabilized assets where mark-to-market upside is limited.

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwriting should assume a wider distribution of rate outcomes (including fewer cuts and a longer period of elevated financing costs).
  • Deals depending on aggressive refinancing proceeds in 2026–2027 remain vulnerable if lenders stay conservative on DSCR and debt yields.

2) Rate Futures Started to Price Hike Risk—A Meaningful Signal for Financing Costs

Market pricing shifted fast: Reuters noted futures were pricing roughly a 25% chance of a rate hike by December, a sharp reversal from recent expectations for multiple cuts. (Reuters)

Reuters also highlighted the two-year Treasury yield around 3.89%, about 25 bps above the Fed's effective policy rate—often a sign the bond market is worried about persistent inflation (or at least less confident in near-term easing). (Reuters)

For CRE, this matters most through the forward curve: when lenders and buyers lose confidence in "near-term cuts," spreads and all-in coupons can stay higher for longer, and loan sizing becomes more restrictive.

What it means for CRE:

  • Expect continued lender discipline on proceeds and structure (cash management, reserves, and tighter covenants) until the rate path becomes clearer.
  • For acquisitions: prioritize basis discipline over "rates will save us" assumptions.

3) CMBS Distress Looks Managed—But via Extensions, Not a Clean Reset

Trepp reported the overall CMBS delinquency rate declined 33 bps to 7.14% in February 2026, with office down to 11.20% and retail down to 6.30%. (Trepp)

But the more important point is why it improved: Trepp attributed much of the decline to modifications and extensions on large matured office loans and mall loans. (Trepp)

That pattern is consistent with a market still working through maturity walls with "amend-and-extend" rather than refinance-at-par execution. Extensions can reduce near-term defaults, but they also delay price discovery and keep a pipeline of potential distress in place.

What it means for CRE:

  • Distress may arrive unevenly: extensions can keep assets afloat until a lease rollover, capex requirement, or sponsorship issue forces a sale.
  • Buyers hunting for discounts should monitor extension-heavy subsectors (notably office) because that can turn into supply later.

4) Pasadena Office Sale Shows the Value Reset Is Still Real in SoCal

Commercial Observer reported Irvine Company sold the 270,000 SF Western Asset Plaza at 385 E Colorado Blvd in Pasadena for $97.9M (~$360/SF). (Commercial Observer)

The article notes Irvine bought the asset in 2012 for $144.5M, and frames the trade as a steep discount to prior pricing. (Commercial Observer)

Even with reported occupancy around 90% when a major tenant renewed in 2023, the clearing price signals how much buyers require for office risk today—higher cap rates, higher reserves for tenant improvements/leasing commissions, and more skepticism about long-duration demand. (Commercial Observer)

What it means for CRE:

  • "Stabilized" office is not immune: even leased assets can trade at repriced levels if buyers underwrite rollover risk and higher capex.
  • For owners: the hold/sell decision should be framed as a capital allocation question—do future TI/LC and downtime risk justify redeploying into better risk-adjusted sectors?

5) LA Office Demand: Flight-to-Quality Continues, but Deals Are Smaller

GlobeSt reported Class A space captured a 72.4% leasing share in 2025 (cycle high), consistent with an ongoing flight-to-quality where tenants consolidate but upgrade into amenity-rich buildings. (GlobeSt)

At the same time, GlobeSt noted fewer transactions per month and smaller average deals as occupiers plan around hybrid work and shrinking footprints. (GlobeSt)

This is a critical combination for underwriting: "better buildings win," but total demand may not grow enough to bail out commodity product—especially where vacancy and sublease competition remain high.

What it means for CRE:

  • Class A can outperform, but pricing must still reflect today's capital costs and concession packages.
  • Class B/C assets likely need a clear repositioning strategy (or a basis that can absorb slower lease-up).

What This Means for LA Investors (By Property Type)

  • Multifamily: Underwrite conservatively on refinancing: higher-for-longer rates can keep DSCR-limited proceeds tight. Focus on submarket-level rent resilience and expense control; avoid "quick cap-rate compression" assumptions.
  • Industrial: Prioritize functional assets (clear heights, loading, access) and tenant-credit; higher discount rates put more value on durable cash flow. Watch tenant rollover timing—capital markets volatility can widen spreads even when fundamentals are healthy.
  • Retail: Necessity-based centers may continue to benefit from steadier foot traffic, but financing remains selective. Stress-test for tenant churn and higher TI/LC, even if occupancy is currently strong.
  • Office: Expect continued repricing and "extension-driven" distress: selectively target well-located assets where basis and capex plan are truly realistic. Trophy assets can lease, but the market is signaling higher required returns—especially outside premier nodes.

Oil-Driven Inflation Risk Meets CMBS 'Extend-and-Pretend': What This Week's Macro Signals Mean for LA CRE

Oil's latest spike has reintroduced inflation risk just as markets were leaning toward mid-2026 Fed cuts. In credit, Trepp data shows CMBS delinquency improving headline-wise, but largely via extensions—while LA multifamily fundamentals remain resilient despite flat-to-soft rent growth and a still-active construction pipeline.

Read Full Article →

Oil markets, the Fed's rate path, and commercial real estate credit are colliding again—fast. This week's headlines are a reminder that CRE isn't just about cap rates and rent comps; it's also about inflation expectations, financing availability, and how lenders behave when maturities hit.

Below is what we're watching and how it flows through to Southern California valuations, debt terms, and deal structure.

1) The Fed May Be "On Hold," but the Inflation Narrative Is Shifting Again

Federal Reserve officials are widely expected to keep policy rates unchanged at this week's meeting, with new forecasts likely to reflect a tougher balancing act: war-related energy shocks can slow growth while simultaneously keeping inflation elevated. (Reuters)

Reuters notes that oil prices have surged sharply amid the Iran conflict (and related shipping risk), reintroducing the kind of inflation uncertainty that can keep the Fed cautious even if parts of the economy soften. (Reuters)

What it means for CRE:

  • In a higher-volatility inflation regime, "cuts later" is as impactful as "higher for longer." Even if the next move is still down, lenders often widen spreads and shorten term when uncertainty rises.
  • Expect more conservative underwriting on exit cap rates and rent growth—especially for transitional assets where stabilization depends on a smooth macro backdrop.

2) Rate-Cut Expectations Are Drifting—CRE Should Underwrite to a Wider Range of Outcomes

Many major Wall Street firms still expect rate cuts to begin around mid-2026, but Reuters reports that at least one major shop (Goldman Sachs) pushed its expected start date back (from June to September) as oil-price inflation risk returned to the foreground. (Reuters)

This matters because CRE pricing rarely adjusts only to today's policy rate; it adjusts to the expected path of rates, which influences both buyer bids and lender confidence in refinancing takeouts.

What it means for CRE:

  • Underwrite bridge/business-plan deals with "rate path uncertainty" explicitly (e.g., downside refi proceeds and higher debt yield requirements).
  • Even for stabilized assets, the market can remain choppy if forward curves move around—particularly in office and retail, where credit committees are already cautious.

3) Oil Is Back Above $100—and That Filters Through to Everything from CPI to Operating Expenses

Reuters reported Brent crude around $101 and WTI around $94.91 as renewed attacks and shipping disruptions heightened supply risk, with the Strait of Hormuz (a critical corridor for global oil and LNG trade) again a focal point for markets. (Reuters)

In CRE terms, oil doesn't just matter for "macro." It shows up in transportation, logistics costs, certain building materials and services, and—most importantly—consumer inflation expectations that can change the Fed's reaction function.

What it means for CRE:

  • Industrial and retail are most exposed to transportation-driven cost pressures, but multifamily feels it too via household budget stress (especially at workforce price points).
  • For value-add and development, higher energy costs can amplify construction cost volatility and project contingency requirements.

4) CMBS Distress Improved—Largely Because Loans Got Extended, Not Because Fundamentals Healed

Trepp reports that the overall U.S. CMBS delinquency rate declined 33 bps to 7.14% in February 2026. (Trepp)

But the driver matters: Trepp attributes the improvement primarily to modifications and extensions of large matured office and mall loans—an "extend-and-pretend" dynamic that lowers near-term delinquency prints while keeping longer-term refinancing and valuation risk unresolved. (Trepp)

What it means for CRE:

  • Distress may be delayed—not avoided—particularly in office. For buyers, that can create opportunity, but only with conservative basis and realistic lease-up timelines.
  • For lenders, extensions signal a preference to manage losses over time rather than crystallize them—supporting the case that true price discovery can be slow.

5) LA Multifamily: Resilient Occupancy, Soft Rent Prints, and a Pipeline That Still Matters

A GlobeSt report highlighted a familiar LA pattern: rent growth has flattened, but stabilized occupancy remains strong—reported at 95.9% in November, above the U.S. average—while the construction pipeline is still active. (GlobeSt)

GlobeSt also cited 25,754 units under construction at the end of 2025 and 6,836 units that broke ground, reinforcing that supply is not "zero," even in a high-barrier market. (GlobeSt)

What it means for CRE:

  • Occupancy strength supports income durability, but flat rent growth shifts the value-add playbook: returns depend more on operational execution (expense control, unit turns, ancillary income) than rent spikes.
  • Submarket selection matters more when supply is uneven; deals near durable job nodes, transit, and infill constraints can still outperform.

What This Means for LA Investors (by Property Type)

  • Multifamily: Underwrite to occupancy durability, not aggressive rent growth; prioritize assets where renovations drive resident retention and controlled NOI lift. Stress-test expenses tied to energy/insurance and assume concessions remain a lever in competitive submarkets.
  • Industrial: Expect demand to remain linked to logistics costs and trade patterns; higher fuel costs can change network optimization and tenant location decisions. Favor infill last-mile and functional clear heights/yard where replacement cost and land scarcity support rents.
  • Retail: Focus on necessity-based and well-located neighborhood centers where tenant sales are resilient, but watch consumer budgets if gas/food inflation stays elevated. In underwriting, place more emphasis on tenant credit and lease rollover timing.
  • Office: The credit backdrop (CMBS extensions, elevated delinquency) suggests slow price discovery; be selective and assume longer repositioning timelines. If pursuing office opportunities, prioritize basis, leasing story, and realistic capex.

Fed Holds Rates as Oil Shock Clouds the Outlook, CRE Distress Eases, and Banks Quietly Return to Lending

The Fed held rates steady at 3.50%–3.75% yesterday as Chair Powell warned that surging oil prices from the Iran conflict could complicate the inflation picture. Meanwhile, CRE distress dipped for the first time since mid-2025, banks are cautiously re-entering retail CRE lending, and BMO announced a 130-location California expansion. Here's what LA investors need to know.

Read Full Article →

Fed Holds at 3.50%–3.75% — Powell Warns Oil Prices Could Stall Progress

The Federal Reserve voted 11-1 on March 18 to keep the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%, the second consecutive meeting with no change. The lone dissent came from Stephen Miran, who preferred a quarter-point cut.

Chair Powell's press conference struck a cautious tone. He acknowledged that inflation "isn't coming down as much as we'd hoped" and warned that rising oil prices — Brent crude has surged over 25% since the start of the Iran conflict to around $91 per barrel — could push inflation expectations higher and weigh on economic growth.

Powell declined to use the word "stagflation" but said the Fed faces a difficult balancing act: labor market risks point toward lower rates, while inflation risks argue for holding or even higher rates. He projected inflation would improve by mid-year as tariff effects fade, but cautioned that the Fed's forecast of two rate cuts this year "hinges on economic performance — if we do not observe needed progress, rate cuts will not materialize."

What it means for CRE: The 10-Year Treasury moved above 4.20% following the meeting. With the first rate cut now not expected until September at the earliest, commercial mortgage rates will remain elevated through the first half of 2026. Borrowers facing maturities this year have limited room to wait for relief.

Oil Prices and the Iran Conflict: A New Risk for CRE

The Iran conflict, now in its third week, has emerged as the most significant external risk to the CRE recovery. Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz has disrupted approximately 20% of global oil supplies. Brent crude surged from around $70 to over $91 per barrel, and U.S. gasoline prices have risen above $4 per gallon — the highest since late 2023.

Bank of America raised its 2026 Brent crude forecast to $77.50 from $61, and Goldman Sachs has warned that if disruptions persist, oil could cross $100 per barrel.

What it means for CRE: Higher energy costs flow through to construction materials, tenant operating expenses, and consumer purchasing power. For developers, this means tighter margins on ground-up projects. For retail and hospitality landlords, the risk is reduced consumer spending as households allocate more to fuel. The March and April CPI readings — which will be the first to capture the full oil price spike — will be critical for understanding whether this is a temporary shock or a structural shift in the inflation trajectory.

CRE Distress Rate Dips to 11.6% — But Don't Celebrate Yet

CRED iQ data shows the overall CRE distress rate fell to 11.63% in February, down from a cycle high of 11.98% in January. The CMBS delinquency rate also improved, declining 33 basis points to 7.14%.

Office posted the largest improvement, with its delinquency rate dropping 114 basis points to 11.20% (down from January's all-time high of 12.34%), driven primarily by modifications and extensions on five large matured loans. Retail declined 74 basis points to 6.30%, the lowest since August 2024. Multifamily edged down nine basis points to 6.85%.

CRED iQ projects the distress rate will remain in the 11%–12.5% range through mid-year before beginning a gradual descent in the second half of 2026 — contingent on Treasury rate stability and no material deterioration in employment. Office distress is expected to push toward 13%–14% before finding a floor.

What it means for CRE: The modest improvement is encouraging, but this is a workout cycle, not a recovery. The wall of CMBS and CRE CLO maturities in 2026 — particularly loans originated in 2021–2022 at compressed cap rates — remains the key stress test ahead. Green Street data still shows overall CRE values 16% below their April 2022 peak, with office down 35%.

Banks Quietly Return to Retail CRE Lending

After pulling back from commercial real estate for nearly three years, banks are cautiously re-entering the CRE lending space — particularly for retail properties. According to GlobeSt, banks steadily reduced their CRE exposure from Q1 2023 through Q4 2025, but recent data suggests that trend is reversing.

Separately, BMO announced plans to open over 130 new financial centers in California, with seven locations opening in 2026 across Greater Los Angeles, the Bay Area, and San Diego. The expansion signals confidence in California's commercial real estate market and will drive demand for well-located retail and office space.

Life insurance companies are also increasing their CRE allocations for 2026, driven by lower demand for long-term loans during the rate-hiking cycle and higher demand for annuities from an aging population.

What it means for CRE: More lender competition is positive for borrowers, particularly in the retail sector where fundamentals have been quietly improving. For LA investors, the combination of banks returning to CRE lending and new institutional demand for California commercial space could create favorable financing conditions later this year — assuming the oil shock doesn't derail the recovery.

Consumer Sentiment Hits Lowest Level Since December — Spending Caution Grows

The University of Michigan's preliminary Consumer Sentiment Index for March fell to 55.5, the weakest reading since December, with expectations dropping to 54.1 — the lowest since November. The personal saving rate rose to 4.5%, indicating consumers are pulling back.

Meanwhile, existing home sales rose 1.7% in February to 4.09 million units, beating expectations but still down 1.4% year over year. The median price hit $398,000, with affordability improving as wage growth outpaces home price appreciation. First-time buyers made up 34% of transactions, up from 31% a year ago.

What it means for CRE: The for-sale housing market isn't pulling renters away in meaningful numbers. With transaction volumes well below pre-pandemic norms and homeownership still out of reach for many households, multifamily demand should remain supported. But the cautious consumer weighs on retail and hospitality. The saving rate increase suggests tenants in consumer-facing sectors may face revenue headwinds — something to watch when underwriting lease renewals.

What This Means for LA Investors

The picture this week is one of crosscurrents. The Fed is on hold, oil prices are creating new uncertainty, but the CRE distress cycle may have peaked, and banks are coming back to the lending table. For LA specifically:

  • Multifamily remains well-supported by structural demand, though floating-rate borrowers should be watching SOFR closely as rate cuts get pushed further out.
  • Retail is having a quiet comeback — tighter vacancy, banks willing to lend again, and institutional expansion (BMO) signaling confidence.
  • Office remains the most challenged sector, but modifications and extensions are keeping the worst-case scenarios from materializing.
  • Industrial continues to normalize, with vacancy stable and demand shifting toward specialized manufacturing and last-mile logistics.

Market Update Los Angeles Interest Rates Retail

Week in Review: Cedars-Sinai's $270M Mall Acquisition, Fed Holds Steady, and Developer Optimism Climbs

A landmark retail deal in Beverly Hills, the Federal Reserve's anticipated rate hold this week, rising construction costs from tariffs, and a surprising uptick in developer sentiment — here's everything LA commercial real estate investors need to know this week.

Read Full Article →

Cedars-Sinai Acquires Beverly Connection for $270 Million

The biggest LA deal of the week was Cedars-Sinai Health System's $270 million acquisition of the Beverly Connection, a 341,000-square-foot retail complex at the corner of La Cienega and Beverly Boulevards. The sale, from Ashkenazy Acquisition Corporation, works out to approximately $790 per square foot — a premium that reflects the property's strategic location adjacent to the hospital's main campus.

While Cedars-Sinai has not announced specific redevelopment plans, the acquisition signals a continued trend of healthcare and institutional buyers acquiring well-located commercial properties in LA's Westside corridor. For retail investors, the deal underscores that prime LA locations still command significant valuations even as the broader retail sector faces headwinds. LA retail vacancy stood at 5.6% as of Q4 2025, according to Kidder Mathews, one of the tightest vacancy rates among major metros.

All Eyes on the Fed: Rates Expected to Hold at 3.50%–3.75%

The Federal Reserve's Federal Open Market Committee convenes on March 17–18 for its latest policy meeting, and the consensus is clear: rates are expected to hold at their current 3.50%–3.75% range. While the market has been anticipating further easing after cuts in late 2024, the Fed has signaled a patient approach amid persistent inflation and trade policy uncertainty.

Wall Street forecasts are diverging on the timeline for the next cut. Goldman Sachs recently pushed its rate-cut forecast to September and December of 2026 — a more conservative outlook than earlier projections. Barclays expects just one cut for all of 2026. The 10-Year Treasury yield sat at approximately 4.27% as of March 12, a level that continues to keep commercial mortgage rates elevated.

LA's New Adaptive Reuse Ordinance: Turning Empty Offices Into Apartments

Effective February 1, 2026, Los Angeles adopted the Citywide Adaptive Reuse Ordinance — a sweeping policy that makes it dramatically easier to convert vacant commercial buildings into housing. With office vacancy at 23%, this could reshape the city's real estate landscape.

Read Full Article →

Two Crises, One Solution

Los Angeles faces a paradox that has defined its real estate market for years: a severe housing shortage alongside 50 million square feet of vacant office space. According to Cushman & Wakefield's Greater Los Angeles Office Report, office vacancy stands at roughly 23% — a record high. Meanwhile, the city continues to struggle to meet state-mandated housing production targets.

The Citywide Adaptive Reuse Ordinance (ARO), which went into effect February 1, 2026, is designed to address both problems simultaneously by streamlining the conversion of underutilized commercial buildings into multifamily housing.

What the New Ordinance Allows

The updated ARO is a significant expansion of the original 1999 ordinance that helped spark downtown LA's residential revival. According to an analysis by JD Supra, the key changes include:

  • Citywide eligibility: The ordinance now applies across all of Los Angeles, not just specific designated areas like the original.
  • Lower age threshold: Buildings as young as 15 years old qualify for as-of-right conversion. Buildings 5–15 years old can qualify through a streamlined administrative review.
  • Broader building types: Office buildings, industrial properties, retail stores, and even parking garages are all eligible for conversion to housing.
  • No minimum unit sizes: Developers are exempt from minimum unit size requirements, enabling creative and flexible floor plans.
  • Streamlined approvals: Most qualifying projects can go directly to building permits without lengthy City Council review.

Developers Are Already Moving

The response has been swift. As the Los Angeles Times reported, developer Garrett Lee has already begun converting a gleaming downtown office high-rise into nearly 700 apartments. David Tedesco of IMT Residential is moving forward with plans to transform the former Sunkist Growers headquarters in Sherman Oaks into 95 apartments — a neighborhood that was never included in the prior ordinance.

"This is monumental for the city," Lee told the Times. He plans to use terms in the new ordinance that allow him to put more apartments on each floor of his projects.

The Original ARO Produced Over 12,000 Units

The precedent is encouraging. The 1999 adaptive reuse ordinance, which was primarily limited to older downtown buildings, helped produce more than 12,000 housing units and is widely credited with transforming downtown Los Angeles from a daytime-only commercial district into a vibrant residential neighborhood. The LA Business Journal reports that since 2016, LA has seen 5 million square feet of office space converted or demolished, with an additional 4 million square feet planned or in the pipeline.

What This Means for Commercial Property Owners

For owners of underperforming office, retail, or industrial buildings in Los Angeles, the new ARO presents a significant opportunity. Buildings that have struggled with high vacancy and declining rents now have a clear regulatory pathway to residential conversion — potentially unlocking substantial value. Key advantages include retaining existing floor area and building height even if they exceed current zoning, locking parking at existing levels with surplus spaces convertible to units or amenities, and the ability to add interior courtyards and light wells without floor area penalties.

California's Insurance Crisis Is Hitting Commercial Real Estate Hard — Here's What to Know

Rising premiums, insurer withdrawals, and the exploding FAIR Plan are reshaping the economics of owning commercial property in California. New 2026 legislation offers some relief, but the crisis is far from over.

Read Full Article →

The Crisis in Numbers

California's property insurance market is under severe stress. Major insurers have pulled back from issuing new policies, premiums are spiking, and the state's insurer of last resort — the California FAIR Plan — has seen its exposure surge 37% year-over-year, with policies more than doubling in wildfire-prone zones over the past four years. According to Bloomberg, 14% of FAIR Plan policies now cover properties in urban zones with low fire risk — a sign that the crisis has spread well beyond wildfire zones.

For commercial property owners, the impact is direct: higher operating costs, more difficult financing, and in some cases, an inability to obtain adequate coverage at any price.

Why This Matters for CRE

Insurance availability is a fundamental requirement for commercial real estate transactions. Without adequate coverage, lenders won't finance, buyers can't close, and property values decline. The Reuters 2026 CRE analysis identified rising insurance costs as a potential "deal-breaker" in certain California markets. And a legal analysis from WSHB warns that the FAIR Plan's commercial coverage cap of $20 million per location is "far below replacement costs" for many larger properties — leaving buildings structurally underinsured.

New 2026 Laws Offer Some Relief

California has enacted several new laws effective January 1, 2026, aimed at stabilizing the market. According to the California Department of Insurance, the key provisions include:

  • Business Insurance Protection Act (SB 547): Extends the existing non-renewal moratorium to commercial policies, covering businesses, HOAs, condominiums, affordable housing, and nonprofits — a significant new protection for commercial property owners.
  • FAIR Plan Stability Act (AB 226): Allows the FAIR Plan to access catastrophe bonds and lines of credit, providing additional financial backing for the state's insurer of last resort.
  • Wildfire safety grants and discounts: New programs incentivize fire-resistant improvements with expanded insurance discounts and faster claim payouts.

Additionally, SB 1076, introduced in February, would require insurers to offer coverage to any property meeting wildfire-safety standards starting in 2028 — a potential game-changer if passed.

What Commercial Property Owners Should Do Now

The insurance landscape is evolving rapidly. For commercial property owners in the LA market, proactive steps include:

  • Review your coverage immediately. Ensure your policy limits reflect current replacement costs, not outdated valuations.
  • Invest in fire-resistant improvements. Properties that meet wildfire-safety standards may qualify for better rates and, potentially, guaranteed coverage under pending legislation.
  • Budget for higher insurance costs. Factor realistic insurance projections into NOI calculations for acquisitions and refinancing.
  • Explore surplus lines and alternative markets. Specialty insurers and excess & surplus lines carriers may offer options when standard markets won't.

LA Commercial Real Estate in Spring 2026: Stabilization, Bifurcation, and Where Capital Is Flowing

As we move into Q2 2026, the commercial real estate landscape in Los Angeles — and nationally — is defined by a striking paradox. Record-high office vacancy exists alongside historic demand for industrial, data center, and healthcare real estate. Here's what investors and property owners need to know.

The Big Picture: Stabilization With Caution

Industry experts are calling 2026 a year of "stabilization and recovery" for commercial real estate. According to CNBC's 2026 outlook, citing research from Cushman & Wakefield and CoStar, the U.S. real estate market is entering the year with "renewed energy, clearer visibility, and increasing optimism in both leasing and capital markets."

But the picture is far from uniform. As Lincoln Property Company notes, "extreme distress exists in some sectors, while demand exists in others." The markets thriving in 2026 are those with structural, long-term demand drivers — population migration, technology infrastructure, and essential services — rather than those dependent on pre-pandemic assumptions.

Los Angeles County's office sector entered 2026 with record-high vacancy. According to Marcus & Millichap's Q1 2026 report, both CBD and suburban vacancy hover around 20%, with availability above 17% across all six of the metro's largest submarkets. The overall county office vacancy stands at a staggering 23.4%.

However, there are emerging bright spots. Class B/C office space saw positive net absorption during each quarter of 2025, suggesting that smaller tenants and service-based businesses are re-entering the market. California logged a record number of high-propensity business applications last year, and Los Angeles County historically captures the most filings in the state — a leading indicator for future leasing activity.

Industrial: Normalized but Still Resilient

LA County's industrial vacancy has climbed to 4.6% — the highest in a decade — according to Cushman & Wakefield's Q4 2025 SoCal report. But context matters: rents remain roughly 48% above pre-pandemic levels, and net absorption turned positive on an annual basis for the first time since 2022.

Where Capital Is Flowing in 2026

The most significant trend of 2026, according to Lincoln Property Company's analysis, is capital reallocation toward sectors with durable demand. Three areas stand out:
